Chapter I

Virtual Local Area Networks

Segmenting the Broadcast Domain Without Rewiring the World
Motivation

Why VLANs? The Scaling Problem of a Single Broadcast Domain

Imagine a university campus network—not the idealized diagrams of textbooks, but the real thing, humming and clicking in a basement room, serving thousands of machines across departments of computer science, electrical engineering, mechanical engineering, and the humanities. Every single machine, in the absence of any subdivision, resides in one flat Layer 2 network. This means every broadcast frame—every ARP request asking “who has 192.168.1.1?”, every DHCP discover message, every frame destined for an unknown MAC address—floods out of every switch port on the entire campus. A DHCP request from a laptop in the physics department creates traffic on a switch port serving the library’s printers. An ARP request in the dean’s office consumes bandwidth at a server rack in the basement. This is the reality of a single broadcast domain, and it is the fundamental problem that VLANs were designed to solve.

The issue is not merely one of wasted bandwidth, although that is certainly a concern. In a sufficiently large LAN, broadcast traffic can consume a non-trivial fraction of available capacity. Every host must process every broadcast frame at least enough to determine that the frame is not relevant to it—this consumes CPU cycles on every endpoint. But the more serious problems are ones of security, privacy, and administrative flexibility. In a single broadcast domain, any host can see broadcast traffic from any other host. A compromised machine in one department can inject malicious ARP responses that affect machines in entirely different departments. A curious student running a packet sniffer in promiscuous mode can observe broadcast traffic—and potentially unicast traffic misdirected by ARP poisoning—from across the entire organization. There is no isolation, no containment, no way to say “these machines talk to each other freely, but those machines should not even see each other’s presence at Layer 2.”

Then there is the administrative problem, which is perhaps the most immediately felt by network operators. Consider a computer science professor who moves offices from the CS building to the electrical engineering building. Physically, her computer is now plugged into a switch port in the EE building. But she needs to remain on the CS department’s network—she needs access to CS file servers, CS subnets, CS-specific firewall rules, and CS IP address ranges. In a world without VLANs, her machine is now on the EE network, period. The only solutions are either to physically rewire the building’s switches (impractical and fragile), to deploy a separate physical network infrastructure for each department (expensive and unscalable), or to accept that the user’s network location is determined entirely by her physical location (inflexible and frustrating).

VLANs dissolve this coupling between physical topology and logical network membership. They allow a single physical switch—or an entire network of interconnected switches—to be partitioned into multiple virtual LANs, each of which functions as its own independent broadcast domain. Frames in one VLAN cannot leak into another VLAN at Layer 2, providing isolation, security, and efficient use of bandwidth. And a port’s VLAN membership is a software configuration, not a hardware constraint, meaning that the CS professor’s new office port can be reassigned to the CS VLAN with a few keystrokes on a management console, without any physical rewiring whatsoever.

Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN): A logical grouping of network nodes—defined by software configuration on switch ports, MAC addresses, or protocol types—such that members of the same VLAN share a common broadcast domain, while nodes in different VLANs are isolated from each other at Layer 2, regardless of their physical location or physical connectivity.

Analogy: Think of a large open-plan office. Without VLANs, it’s as if there are no walls—everyone hears every conversation (broadcast). VLANs are like installing glass partitions: people in the same partitioned area can talk freely among themselves, but cannot hear conversations in other areas. The partitions are movable (software-configured), so when someone moves desks, you just reposition the glass—no construction needed.

VLAN vs. Subnet: A VLAN operates at Layer 2 (Data Link), segmenting broadcast domains. A subnet operates at Layer 3 (Network), segmenting IP address ranges. In practice, each VLAN typically maps to a separate IP subnet, but they are conceptually distinct. A VLAN prevents broadcast frames from crossing boundaries; a subnet determines how IP packets are routed. You could theoretically have multiple subnets within one VLAN, but this is poor practice because broadcasts still cross subnet boundaries within the VLAN.

Core Concept

Port-Based VLANs: The Simplest and Most Common Form

The most widely deployed form of VLAN is the port-based VLAN, and its mechanism is elegantly straightforward. A switch that supports VLAN capabilities—which is to say, virtually every managed switch manufactured in the last two decades—allows its physical ports to be grouped into one or more VLANs using the switch’s management software. When a port is assigned to a particular VLAN, the switch treats that port as belonging to an isolated broadcast domain consisting only of the other ports in the same VLAN. A frame arriving on a port in VLAN 10 will only be forwarded out of other ports that also belong to VLAN 10. The switch maintains a separate forwarding table for each VLAN, so even MAC address learning is VLAN-aware: if a frame with source MAC AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF arrives on a port in VLAN 10, the switch learns that AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF is reachable via VLAN 10, not via any other VLAN.

Consider a concrete example. A 16-port switch is configured so that ports 1 through 8 belong to the EE VLAN and ports 9 through 15 belong to the CS VLAN. Port 16 might be a special uplink port. When a host on port 2 (EE VLAN) sends a broadcast frame, the switch forwards that frame only out of ports 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8—the other ports in the EE VLAN. Ports 9 through 15 see nothing. The CS hosts are completely unaware that a broadcast occurred in the EE VLAN. This is traffic isolation, and it is the fundamental benefit of VLANs.

It is worth noting that port-based VLANs are not the only possible implementation. Some switches support MAC-based VLANs, where VLAN membership is determined by the MAC address of the endpoint rather than the physical port. This means that a laptop belongs to a particular VLAN regardless of which switch port it plugs into. This is more flexible but also more complex to manage, since the switch must maintain a mapping of MAC addresses to VLANs. There are also protocol-based VLANs, where VLAN membership is determined by the Layer 3 protocol in the frame (IP, IPX, AppleTalk, etc.), though these are largely historical. Finally, some systems support dynamic VLAN membership, where ports can be assigned to VLANs on the fly based on authentication (for example, via IEEE 802.1X), allowing a user’s VLAN assignment to follow them based on their login credentials rather than their physical port or MAC address.

Figure 1: Port-Based VLAN — A single physical switch partitioned into two isolated broadcast domains. EE hosts (tan) can broadcast to each other but not to CS hosts (green), and vice versa.
Key Properties of Port-Based VLANs
  • Traffic Isolation: Frames from ports in VLAN X can only reach other ports in VLAN X. The switch enforces this isolation in hardware.
  • Separate Forwarding Tables: Each VLAN has its own MAC address table, so the same MAC address could theoretically appear in different VLANs without conflict.
  • Software Configuration: VLAN membership is configured via the switch management interface (CLI, SNMP, or web GUI)—no physical rewiring needed.
  • Flexible Membership: Ports can be dynamically reassigned between VLANs. Some implementations support MAC-based or authentication-based VLAN assignment.
Inter-Switch

VLANs Spanning Multiple Switches: The Trunk Port

Port-based VLANs on a single switch are conceptually simple, but real networks rarely consist of a single switch. A building might have one switch per floor, and departments might span multiple floors. The CS department might have machines on the first, third, and fifth floors, each connected to a different switch. We need a mechanism that allows VLANs to extend across multiple switches while maintaining traffic isolation. This is where the trunk port enters the picture.

A trunk port is a switch port configured to carry traffic for multiple VLANs between switches. Unlike an access port, which carries traffic for only a single VLAN and connects to end hosts, a trunk port sits on the inter-switch link and must be able to distinguish which VLAN each frame belongs to. This creates an immediate problem: an ordinary Ethernet frame (as defined by IEEE 802.1) contains no VLAN identifier. If switch A sends a frame from VLAN 10 and a frame from VLAN 20 to switch B over the same trunk link, switch B has no way to tell which VLAN each frame belongs to. The frames look identical at the Ethernet layer. To solve this, we need a protocol that tags frames with their VLAN identifiers as they traverse trunk links, and removes those tags when frames are forwarded out of access ports to end hosts.

That protocol is IEEE 802.1Q, and it is the industry standard for VLAN tagging. When a frame enters a switch through an access port belonging to VLAN 10, the switch associates the frame with VLAN 10 internally. When the switch needs to forward that frame out of a trunk port, it inserts a 4-byte 802.1Q tag into the Ethernet frame header. This tag contains, among other fields, a 12-bit VLAN identifier that explicitly labels the frame as belonging to VLAN 10. When the neighboring switch receives the frame on its trunk port, it reads the VLAN identifier, strips the tag, and forwards the frame only out of ports that belong to VLAN 10. The tag exists only on the trunk link—end hosts never see it. This insertion-and-removal process is entirely handled by the switches and is transparent to the connected endpoints.

Protocol Deep Dive

IEEE 802.1Q: The VLAN Frame Format

The 802.1Q standard, formally known as IEEE 802.1Q Virtual Bridged Local Area Networks, defines how VLAN information is encoded within Ethernet frames. The mechanism is both elegant and minimally disruptive: a 4-byte tag is inserted between the source MAC address and the EtherType (or Length) field of the standard Ethernet frame. This insertion increases the maximum Ethernet frame size from 1518 bytes to 1522 bytes. The tag consists of two 2-byte fields: the Tag Protocol Identifier (TPID) and the Tag Control Information (TCI).

The TPID is set to the fixed value 0x8100 (81-00 in hexadecimal), which serves as an unambiguous marker that this frame carries an 802.1Q tag. When a switch receives a frame and sees 0x8100 in the position where it expects an EtherType field, it knows that the next two bytes contain VLAN information rather than a protocol identifier. This is a clever piece of engineering: the tag is backward-compatible in the sense that older devices that do not understand 802.1Q will simply see an unfamiliar EtherType and drop the frame, rather than misinterpreting its contents.

The TCI field contains three sub-fields. The most important is the 12-bit VLAN Identifier (VID), which can represent 4,096 distinct VLAN values (0 through 4,095). VLAN 0 is reserved for priority tagging (frames that carry priority information but no VLAN assignment), and VLAN 4,095 (0xFFF) is reserved, so the usable range is VLANs 1 through 4,094. VLAN 1 is typically the default VLAN on most switches. The TCI also contains a 3-bit Priority Code Point (PCP), which functions similarly to the IP ToS/DSCP field and allows Ethernet frames to be assigned one of eight priority levels for Quality of Service (QoS) processing. The remaining bit is the Canonical Format Indicator (CFI), which was used for compatibility between Ethernet and Token Ring networks but is now largely obsolete.

When a switch inserts the 802.1Q tag, the original frame’s Frame Check Sequence (FCS, commonly called CRC) becomes invalid because the frame contents have changed. The switch must therefore recompute the CRC based on the modified frame and insert the new FCS at the end. When the receiving switch strips the tag, it recomputes the CRC again for the original frame. This dual recomputation ensures frame integrity is maintained throughout the tagging and untagging process.

Figure 3: 802.1Q Frame Format — The 4-byte tag is inserted between Source MAC and EtherType. TPID (0x8100) identifies the frame as tagged. TCI carries the 12-bit VLAN ID and 3-bit priority. CRC is recomputed.

For exams: When asked to draw the 802.1Q frame format, you MUST include: (1) TPID = 0x8100, (2) 12-bit VLAN ID within TCI, (3) 3-bit priority (PCP), (4) the fact that CRC is recomputed. Label each field with its size in bytes or bits. This is a very common 5-mark and 10-mark question.

  6. Receiving switch strips tag: When the neighboring switch receives the frame on its trunk port, it reads the VLAN ID (10), removes the 802.1Q tag, recomputes the CRC for the original frame, and forwards the now-untagged frame out of the appropriate access port in VLAN 10.
  7. End host receives standard frame: The destination host receives a perfectly normal Ethernet frame, completely unaware that 802.1Q tagging occurred.
Advanced

VXLAN and EVPN: Stretching Layer 2 Across Layer 3 Networks

The 802.1Q VLAN framework works beautifully within a single building or campus, where switches are connected by direct physical links. But what happens when an organization needs to extend a Layer 2 domain across data centers separated by hundreds or thousands of kilometers? Perhaps a virtual machine needs to be migrated from a data center in Mumbai to one in Chennai without changing its IP address. Perhaps a disaster recovery site needs to maintain an exact Layer 2 replica of the primary site’s network. In these scenarios, the physical infrastructure between the sites is an IP network—a Layer 3 network—and standard 802.1Q trunking cannot operate across it because trunk links require direct Layer 2 connectivity between switches.

This is the problem that VXLAN (Virtual eXtensible LAN) solves. Defined in RFC 7348, VXLAN is a tunneling scheme that overlays Layer 2 Ethernet networks on top of Layer 3 IP networks. The key insight is to encapsulate entire Ethernet frames within IP-UDP datagrams, allowing them to traverse any IP network as if they were simply payload data. The IP network becomes an “underlay” that provides raw connectivity, while the VXLAN tunnels form an “overlay” that preserves Layer 2 semantics. A device called a VXLAN Tunnel Endpoint (VTEP) sits at each end of the tunnel, performing the encapsulation and decapsulation.

VXLAN also solves a scaling limitation of 802.1Q VLANs. The 12-bit VLAN ID field supports only 4,096 VLANs, which is insufficient for large cloud providers who may need to isolate traffic for millions of tenants. VXLAN uses a 24-bit VNID (VXLAN Network Identifier), supporting up to 16 million distinct virtual networks. This massive expansion of the identifier space is one of the primary reasons VXLAN has become the de facto standard for network virtualization in modern data centers and cloud environments.

EVPN (Ethernet VPN), sometimes used interchangeably with VXLAN in informal discussion, is the control plane for VXLAN. While basic VXLAN relies on flooding and learning (similar to how a plain Ethernet switch learns MAC addresses), EVPN uses MP-BGP (Multi-Protocol Border Gateway Protocol) to distribute MAC and IP address information across VTEPs. This eliminates the need for flooding-based MAC learning in the overlay, making the system more scalable and robust. When we speak of “VXLAN with EVPN,” we are referring to the combination of VXLAN data plane encapsulation with EVPN control plane signaling—a powerful and increasingly standard architecture for modern data center networking.

Chapter II

Link Virtualization

A Network as a Link Layer — Treating an Entire Network as a Single Link

The concept of link virtualization represents one of the more philosophically interesting ideas in networking: the notion that an entire network—with its routers, links, protocols, and complexities—can be made to appear to higher layers as if it were simply a single link. This is not merely an abstraction for the sake of elegant layering diagrams; it is a practical engineering strategy that has been deployed at massive scale in both MPLS networks and data center fabrics. The core idea is that the internal structure of a network—how its packets are routed, what protocols it runs, how it handles congestion—can be hidden behind an interface that presents the network as a simple point-to-point or multipoint link to the devices connected to its edges.

Consider what a link means in the traditional sense: a physical medium (copper, fiber, wireless spectrum) that carries frames between two nodes. A link has a maximum frame size (MTU), it may introduce errors, and it delivers frames from one end to the other. Now consider a network of routers connected by links, running IP routing protocols. From the perspective of a device connected to the edge of this network, the network’s behavior can be made to resemble a link: frames (or packets) go in one end and come out the other, with some delay and possibly some loss, but without the edge device needing to understand or participate in the network’s internal routing. The network, in effect, becomes the link.

This perspective helps unify several technologies that might otherwise seem unrelated. MPLS, as we shall see, creates label-switched paths through a network that function like virtual circuits—like links established through the network fabric. VXLAN creates tunnels through an IP network that function like virtual Ethernet links. Even a simple GRE tunnel between two routers creates a virtual link over an IP infrastructure. In each case, the pattern is the same: a complex network is abstracted behind a simpler interface, and the resulting virtual link can be used as a building block in higher-layer network architectures. This is why the syllabus frames this section as “Link Virtualization: A Network as a Link Layer”—it is describing a general principle that MPLS and VXLAN both instantiate in different ways.

The motivation for link virtualization is both technical and economic. Technically, it allows network architects to create virtual topologies that are independent of the physical topology. A virtual link can span multiple physical hops, can be rerouted without disrupting the endpoints, and can carry traffic with specific QoS guarantees. Economically, it allows multiple virtual networks to share the same physical infrastructure, reducing capital expenditure. A single physical network can simultaneously support multiple virtual links carrying different types of traffic, each with its own service level agreement, all multiplexed onto the same fibers and routers.

Link Virtualization: The technique of abstracting an entire network infrastructure (routers, links, protocols) behind an interface that presents the network as a single logical link to connected devices, enabling the creation of virtual topologies independent of physical topology and allowing multiple virtual networks to share the same physical infrastructure.

The network becomes the link. The complex internal routing, the multiple hops, the congestion management—all of it is hidden behind the simple abstraction of “frames go in here and come out there.” This is not a simplification; it is a deliberate and powerful engineering decision.
Examples of Link Virtualization
  • MPLS LSPs: A label-switched path through a network of MPLS routers functions as a virtual link from ingress to egress.
  • VXLAN tunnels: An IP-UDP tunnel between VTEPs functions as a virtual Ethernet link across an IP underlay.
  • GRE tunnels: Generic Routing Encapsulation creates virtual point-to-point links over IP networks.
  • IPsec tunnels: Secure virtual links over public IP networks.
  • Data center fabrics: The entire leaf-spine fabric can be treated as a single logical switch (a single link-layer domain) from the perspective of connected servers.
Chapter III

Multiprotocol Label Switching

Forwarding by Label, Not by Prefix — Speed, Flexibility, and Traffic Engineering
Foundation

The Problem MPLS Solves: Why Prefix Matching Isn’t Enough

To understand MPLS, we must first understand the bottleneck it was designed to address. In traditional IP routing, every router that receives a packet must examine the packet’s destination IP address and perform a longest prefix match against its forwarding table. This means the router compares the destination address against every entry in its table, finding the entry with the longest matching prefix, and forwards the packet accordingly. In the late 1990s, when routing tables were growing rapidly and router hardware was far less capable than today, this lookup operation was computationally expensive. Every packet, at every hop, required this relatively complex table search. For a router processing millions of packets per second, the cumulative cost was significant.

MPLS was conceived as a solution to this performance problem. The idea was borrowed from the world of virtual circuits (as used in ATM and Frame Relay networks), where each packet carries a short, fixed-length identifier—a label—that can be looked up in a simple table with a single exact match, rather than a complex longest-prefix-match search. In MPLS, when a packet enters the MPLS domain at an ingress label-switched router (LSR), the router attaches a label to the packet. Subsequent routers within the MPLS domain forward the packet based solely on this label, without examining the IP header at all. At the egress LSR, the label is removed and the packet resumes its normal IP-forwarded journey. The path that the packet follows through the MPLS domain is called a Label-Switched Path (LSP).

Here is a critical point that students often misunderstand: the IP datagram still keeps its IP address. MPLS does not replace IP. The IP header with source and destination addresses remains intact inside the labeled packet. MPLS routers in the middle of the LSP do not need to look at the IP header because the label alone is sufficient for forwarding. But the IP addresses are still there, preserved for use once the packet exits the MPLS domain. MPLS is not an alternative to IP; it is a supplement that sits between Layer 2 and Layer 3, often called “Layer 2.5.”

As router hardware evolved with the development of ASICs and TCAMs (Ternary Content-Addressable Memory), the original performance motivation for MPLS became less compelling—modern routers can perform longest-prefix matching at line rate. But by the time this happened, MPLS had acquired a second, arguably more important, reason to exist: traffic engineering. In traditional IP routing, the path a packet takes is determined solely by the destination address and the shortest-path routing algorithm. All packets to the same destination take the same path, regardless of source, regardless of current network load, regardless of any policy considerations. MPLS allows network operators to establish paths based on source and destination, on available bandwidth, on administrative policy, on cost—on any criterion they choose. This flexibility, impossible with destination-based IP routing alone, is the primary reason MPLS remains widely deployed in ISP and enterprise backbones today.

Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS): A high-performance packet-forwarding technology that uses short, fixed-length labels inserted between Layer 2 and Layer 3 headers to make forwarding decisions. MPLS routers (Label-Switched Routers, LSRs) forward packets based on label values rather than IP destination prefix matching, enabling faster lookups and flexible traffic engineering. The IP header is preserved but not examined during transit through the MPLS domain.

MPLS Does NOT Replace IP: A common misconception is that MPLS is an alternative to IP routing. In reality, MPLS coexists with IP. The IP header remains in the packet throughout the MPLS domain. MPLS simply provides an alternative forwarding mechanism (label-based) that can coexist with or override IP-based forwarding decisions. Think of MPLS as an overlay on top of IP—it uses the IP infrastructure but makes its own forwarding decisions based on labels.

Architecture

Label-Switched Routers and MPLS Forwarding

An MPLS-capable router is called a Label-Switched Router (LSR). Within an MPLS domain, there are three types of LSRs. The ingress LSR is the router at the edge of the MPLS domain where packets enter. It receives unlabeled IP packets, determines which LSP the packet should follow, pushes (inserts) the appropriate MPLS label onto the packet, and forwards it. The interior (transit) LSR sits inside the MPLS domain and forwards labeled packets based on their label values, performing label swapping—replacing the incoming label with a different outgoing label. The egress LSR is the router at the edge where packets leave the MPLS domain; it pops (removes) the label and forwards the packet using normal IP routing.

The forwarding table used by LSRs is distinct from the IP forwarding table. It is called the Label Forwarding Information Base (LFIB), and it maps incoming labels to outgoing labels and outgoing interfaces. When an interior LSR receives a labeled packet, it looks up the incoming label in its LFIB, finds the corresponding outgoing label and interface, swaps the label, and forwards the packet. This operation—exact-match lookup on a fixed-length label—is inherently simpler than longest-prefix matching on a variable-length IP address, which was the original performance advantage of MPLS. The LFIB is populated by MPLS signaling protocols, not by IP routing protocols, which means MPLS forwarding decisions can be made independently of IP routing decisions.

This independence is the foundation of MPLS traffic engineering. Because the LFIB is separate from the IP routing table, an LSR can be configured to forward packets along paths that differ from what IP routing would choose. For example, IP routing might send all traffic to destination D via the shortest path through router R3. But MPLS can establish two LSPs to D: one through R3 for traffic from source A, and another through R5 and R6 for traffic from source B. Both LSPs reach the same destination, but they take different paths based on the source address—something that pure destination-based IP routing cannot do. Additionally, MPLS supports fast reroute: backup LSPs can be pre-computed so that if a link or node fails, traffic can be immediately diverted to the backup path without waiting for IP routing protocols to converge. This can reduce failover times from seconds (typical IP convergence) to milliseconds.

Signaling

MPLS Signaling: How Labels Get Distributed

For MPLS forwarding to work, every LSR along a label-switched path must have the correct label mappings in its LFIB. This requires a mechanism for distributing labels and establishing LSPs. There are two primary components to this process: the routing protocol extensions that carry MPLS-relevant information, and the signaling protocol that sets up the LSPs themselves.

The first component involves extending existing link-state routing protocols—specifically OSPF and IS-IS—to carry additional information relevant to MPLS traffic engineering. Standard OSPF and IS-IS flood link-state information that describes the topology of the network: which routers are connected, what the link costs are, and so on. The MPLS extensions add information about link bandwidth—how much total bandwidth a link has, how much is currently reserved for MPLS LSPs, and how much is available. This information allows the ingress LSR to compute paths through the network that have sufficient available bandwidth, rather than simply choosing the shortest path. The modified protocols are called OSPF-TE and IS-IS-TE (the TE suffix stands for Traffic Engineering).

The second component is the RSVP-TE (Resource ReSerVation Protocol with Traffic Engineering) signaling protocol. When the ingress LSR has computed a path for a new LSP (using the bandwidth and topology information obtained from OSPF-TE or IS-IS-TE), it uses RSVP-TE to set up the LSP by sending signaling messages along the computed path. Each router along the path receives the RSVP-TE message, allocates a label for this LSP, installs the label mapping in its LFIB, and forwards the message to the next router. When the egress LSR responds, the labels are confirmed and the LSP is established. RSVP-TE also supports resource reservation—it can reserve bandwidth along the LSP, ensuring that the promised QoS is available.

This two-part architecture—link-state protocols for information distribution, RSVP-TE for LSP establishment—is the classic MPLS control plane. It is worth noting that there is an alternative label distribution protocol called LDP (Label Distribution Protocol), which is simpler but does not support traffic engineering. LDP establishes LSPs that follow the same paths as IP routing, providing the label-switching performance benefit without the traffic engineering flexibility. For the purposes of this course and examination, RSVP-TE is the more important protocol because it enables the traffic engineering capabilities that are MPLS’s primary modern justification.

Forwarding Tables

MPLS Forwarding Tables: The LFIB in Action

The MPLS forwarding table, the Label Forwarding Information Base (LFIB), is conceptually straightforward. Each entry maps an incoming label to an outgoing label and an outgoing interface. When an interior LSR receives a labeled packet, it looks up the incoming label, swaps it with the outgoing label specified in the LFIB entry, and forwards the packet out of the specified interface. This is a “swap” operation. At the penultimate LSR (the router just before the egress), a “pop” operation may be performed, removing the label so that the egress LSR receives a plain IP packet. This optimization, called penultimate hop popping (PHP), saves the egress LSR from having to perform two lookups (one on the label and one on the IP address). At the ingress LSR, a “push” operation adds the first label to an unlabeled IP packet.

MPLS Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages:

  • Faster forwarding than prefix matching (historically)
  • Traffic engineering: paths based on source, destination, policy, bandwidth
  • Fast reroute: pre-computed backup paths for rapid failover
  • Support for QoS and guaranteed bandwidth
  • Layer 2.5 position enables multi-protocol support
  • VPN support (MPLS L3VPN, L2VPN)

Disadvantages:

  • Added complexity: additional protocols (RSVP-TE, LDP)
  • Requires MPLS-capable routers throughout the domain
  • Debugging and troubleshooting are more difficult
  • Performance advantage less relevant with modern hardware
  • Label space is limited per router
  • Not needed in small networks

How to Structure a 10-Mark Answer on MPLS: (1) Begin with the problem: IP longest-prefix matching is slow and path-rigid. (2) Define MPLS formally. (3) Explain labels, LSRs, LSPs. (4) Draw the MPLS vs IP path diagram. (5) Explain the LFIB and swap/pop/push operations. (6) Discuss traffic engineering as the key advantage. (7) Explain signaling: OSPF-TE/IS-IS-TE + RSVP-TE. (8) Mention fast reroute. (9) Advantages and disadvantages. (10) Conclude with MPLS’s position as “Layer 2.5” and its relationship to SDN.

MPLS Quick Revision:

  • MPLS = fixed-length label forwarding (not longest-prefix match)
  • Label inserted between L2 and L3 (“Layer 2.5”)
  • IP addresses preserved but not examined during MPLS transit
  • LSR types: ingress (push), interior (swap), egress (pop)
  • LFIB: incoming label → outgoing label + interface
  • Traffic engineering: source+destination based routing
  • Fast reroute: pre-computed backup LSPs (ms failover)
  • Signaling: OSPF-TE/IS-IS-TE (topology info) + RSVP-TE (LSP setup)
  • MPLS predates SDN by ~10 years; early form of generalized forwarding
Chapter IV

Data Center Networking

Architecting for Scale: Racks, Fabrics, and the Intelligence of the Network
Context

The Scale of Modern Data Centers

A modern data center is an engineering marvel that dwarfs anything the early internet architects could have imagined. Tens of thousands—sometimes hundreds of thousands—of servers are densely packed into rows of racks inside vast, climate-controlled facilities. Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Facebook, Apple, and dozens of other companies operate data centers at scales that were inconceivable even twenty years ago. A single data center might serve millions of concurrent users, host thousands of different applications, and process exabytes of data. The networking challenges posed by this scale are unique and severe, and they have driven an entirely new branch of networking research and practice: data center networking.

The challenges are multifaceted. First, there is the challenge of sheer scale: how do you build a network that efficiently connects 100,000 servers? Traditional hierarchical network designs break down at this scale. Second, there is multi-tenancy and application diversity: multiple applications, each serving massive numbers of clients, must coexist on the same physical infrastructure without interfering with each other. Third, reliability is paramount: a data center serving millions of users cannot afford outages, and the network must be resilient to failures of individual switches, links, servers, and even entire racks. Fourth, load balancing must be sophisticated: processing, networking, and data bottlenecks must be identified and avoided in real time, distributing workloads across available resources while minimizing latency for the end user. Fifth, there is the challenge of data locality: related services and data must be placed as close to each other as possible—ideally within the same rack, or at least in nearby racks—because crossing multiple tiers of the network adds both latency and congestion risk. When a web server needs to fetch data from a storage system or a caching layer, every network hop adds microseconds; at the scale of millions of requests per second, those microseconds compound into a significant performance penalty.

These challenges have driven a fundamental rethinking of network architecture. Traditional enterprise networks, built on hierarchical three-tier designs (access, aggregation, core), were never intended for the east-west traffic patterns that dominate data centers. In a data center, the vast majority of traffic flows between servers within the same facility—server-to-server communication for distributed computing, storage replication, and microservice interactions. This “east-west” traffic pattern demands a network fabric with high bisection bandwidth and low, predictable latency between any two servers, regardless of their physical location. The architectures that have emerged to meet these demands are the subject of this chapter.

Architecture

Data Center Network Elements: Racks, TORs, and the Fabric

The fundamental building block of a data center network is the server rack. A typical rack contains between 20 and 40 server blades—thin, horizontally-mounted computers designed for dense stacking. Each blade is a complete server with its own CPU, memory, storage, and network interface. At the top of this rack sits a switch that connects to every server blade within the rack: this is the Top of Rack (TOR) switch. The TOR is the server’s gateway to the rest of the data center. It typically provides 100G to 400G Ethernet connections down to the server blades and high-speed uplinks (often 400G or more) to the next tier of switches.

Above the TOR switches sits the Tier-2 (aggregation) switch layer. Each Tier-2 switch connects to multiple TOR switches below it, aggregating their traffic and providing connectivity between racks. The number of TOR switches that a single Tier-2 switch can connect to depends on the port density of the switch, but a typical ratio is around 16 TORs per Tier-2 switch. Above the Tier-2 layer, there may be a Tier-1 (core) switch layer that interconnects the Tier-2 switches and provides connectivity to the external internet. However, modern data center designs increasingly flatten this hierarchy, reducing or eliminating the core layer in favor of a more meshed, interconnected fabric.

The key architectural shift in modern data centers is from the rigid, tree-like hierarchy of traditional enterprise networks to a richly interconnected fabric. In a traditional tree, there is only one path between any two servers (up the tree and back down), which creates bottleneck links near the root and single points of failure. In a fabric, there are multiple paths between any pair of TOR switches, providing both increased throughput (because traffic can be spread across multiple paths) and increased reliability (because the failure of any single link or switch only eliminates some paths, not all of them). This rich interconnection is the defining characteristic of modern data center topologies.

Figure 6: Data Center Leaf-Spine Fabric — Every TOR (leaf) connects to every Spine switch, creating multiple equal-cost paths and eliminating single points of failure.
Why Rich Interconnection (Leaf-Spine)?
  • Increased throughput: Multiple paths between any two TORs means aggregate bandwidth scales with the number of spine switches.
  • Increased reliability: Redundancy at every layer. A spine switch failure only reduces available bandwidth; it does not partition the network.
  • Predictable latency: Every inter-rack flow crosses exactly two hops (TOR → Spine → TOR), unlike tree topologies where hop count varies.
  • Scalability: Adding capacity is straightforward: add more spines to increase bisection bandwidth, or add more leaf-spine pods.

Traditional Tree vs. Leaf-Spine: In a traditional three-tier tree (access/aggregation/core), adding servers at the edge eventually oversubscribs the links at the aggregation and core layers, creating bottlenecks. A leaf-spine fabric is explicitly designed to minimize or eliminate oversubscription between tiers, ensuring that any server can communicate with any other server at line rate, limited only by the number of spine uplinks.

Application Layer

Application-Layer Routing: The Role of the Load Balancer

While the physical and link-layer topology provides the raw connectivity within a data center, the intelligent direction of traffic is often handled at the application layer by a critical component known as the load balancer. A load balancer sits at the edge of the data center, between the external internet and the internal server fleet. It receives incoming client requests—HTTP requests for a web page, API calls for a mobile application, database queries—and distributes them across the available backend servers based on factors like server health, current load, geographic proximity, and application logic. To the external client, the data center appears as a single endpoint; the internal complexity of hundreds or thousands of servers is completely hidden.

The load balancer performs several functions beyond simple traffic distribution. It performs health checking: continuously monitoring backend servers and removing unhealthy ones from the rotation. It provides session persistence (affinity): ensuring that a client’s requests are consistently directed to the same backend server for the duration of a session, which is necessary for applications that maintain local state. It terminates TLS/SSL connections, offloading the computationally expensive encryption and decryption work from the backend servers. And it provides a layer of security, absorbing DDoS attacks and filtering malicious requests before they reach the application servers. Without load balancers, the massive horizontal scalability that characterizes modern data center applications would be impossible; there would be no mechanism to coordinate client requests across a fleet of identical servers.

Load Balancer: An application-layer device or software that distributes incoming network traffic across multiple backend servers to optimize resource utilization, maximize throughput, reduce latency, and ensure fault tolerance. It hides the internal architecture of the data center from external clients.

Protocol Innovations

Protocol Innovations Inside the Data Center

The unique requirements of data center networking have driven the development of specialized protocols and adaptations of existing protocols that are rarely seen outside this context. The tightly controlled environment of a data center—where the organization owns every link, switch, and server—allows for optimizations that would be impossible on the open internet.

At the link layer, one of the most significant innovations is RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet). RDMA (Remote Direct Memory Access) allows one machine to directly access the memory of another machine without involving the operating system kernel on either side, dramatically reducing latency and CPU overhead. Originally developed for InfiniBand networks (which have their own specialized link layer), RoCE adapts RDMA to run over standard Ethernet, making it accessible in commodity data center environments. RoCE requires a lossless Ethernet fabric, typically achieved through Priority-based Flow Control (PFC), which prevents packet drops due to buffer overflow. This is critical because RDMA operates under the assumption that the network does not drop packets; retransmission would defeat the latency benefits.

At the transport layer, data centers have developed specialized congestion control mechanisms. Traditional TCP congestion control (like Reno or Cubic) was designed for the wide-area internet, where bandwidth is variable and round-trip times are long. Inside a data center, round-trip times are measured in microseconds, and the standard TCP mechanisms can be too slow to react to congestion. ECN (Explicit Congestion Notification) is used to signal congestion before packets are dropped: switches mark packets with a Congestion Experienced (CE) codepoint when queues approach capacity, and the receiving endpoint echoes this signal back to the sender, which reduces its sending rate. This approach is used by data center TCP variants like DCTCP (Data Center TCP) and DCQCN (Data Center QCN) (the latter for RoCE/RDMA traffic), which respond to ECN signals more aggressively and precisely than standard TCP. There is also experimentation with hop-by-hop (backpressure) congestion control, where switches directly signal their upstream neighbors to reduce sending rates, preventing congestion from building up in the first place.

SDN in the Data Center: Google ORION

Perhaps the most profound development in data center networking is the adoption of Software-Defined Networking (SDN). SDN separates the network’s control plane (which decides how packets are forwarded) from the data plane (which actually forwards them), centralizing control in a software controller that can program the forwarding behavior of all switches in the network.

A landmark example is Google’s ORION SDN control plane, which manages both Google’s internal data center network (called Jupiter) and their wide-area network (called B4). ORION implements routing, traffic engineering, and management as applications running on top of a centralized SDN core. Key aspects include:

  • Intradomain routing and iBGP: Implemented as SDN applications rather than distributed protocols running on each router.
  • Traffic engineering: Edge-to-edge flow-based controls (such as CoFlow scheduling) to meet contractual SLAs.
  • Management: Uses a publish-subscribe distributed microservice architecture within the Orion core.
  • Switch signaling: OpenFlow is used to program and monitor the forwarding tables of switches from the central controller.

This architecture raises a provocative question: Are protocols dying? When routing decisions, congestion control (partially), and failover are all managed by a centralized SDN controller rather than by distributed protocols running on each device, the traditional role of networking protocols is fundamentally diminished. The intelligence moves from the network devices to the software that controls them.

With SDN, the network ceases to be a collection of independently-routing devices and becomes a single, programmable system. The question is no longer “what protocol should this router run?” but “what behavior should the controller program into the fabric?”
Chapter V

Trends in Data Center Networking

AI Fabrics, Edge Computing, and the Sustainable Data Center

Data center networking is not a static discipline. The demands placed upon these networks evolve rapidly, driven by shifts in application architecture, user expectations, and global technological trends. Understanding the current trajectory of data center networking is essential not just for examinations, but for grasping the future of the field itself. The following five trends represent the most significant forces shaping data center networking today.

1. AI and High-Performance Computing (HPC) Focus

The explosive growth of generative AI and large-scale machine learning has fundamentally altered the requirements of data center networks. Training a large language model like GPT-4 requires distributing computation across thousands of GPUs, which must exchange massive volumes of data (gradients and model parameters) with extremely low latency. A single bottleneck in the network can stall the entire training process, making network performance a critical determinant of training efficiency.

This has driven the adoption of 400G and 800G Ethernet, InfiniBand (which offers native RDMA and lossless delivery), and specialized AI accelerator fabrics (such as NVIDIA’s NVLink and NVSwitch). The network in an AI data center is not just a connectivity layer; it is a critical component of the computing fabric itself.

2. Network Automation and SDN

Manual configuration of network devices is slow, error-prone, and fundamentally incompatible with the scale and dynamism of modern data centers. Software-Defined Networking (SDN) and intelligent, automated, and self-optimizing network architectures are replacing manual configuration. Network Intent Abstraction allows operators to declare what they want the network to do, and the SDN controller figures out how to program the devices to achieve it. This increases deployment speed, reduces human error, and enables rapid adaptation to changing conditions. The network becomes code, managed by software engineering practices rather than CLI commands.

3. Edge Computing Growth

Not all data processing can or should happen in centralized data centers. Applications like autonomous driving, industrial IoT, and real-time 5G services require processing latencies of a few milliseconds—latencies that are physically impossible to achieve when the nearest data center is hundreds of kilometers away. Edge data centers—smaller facilities deployed closer to the data source—are increasingly being built to handle these latency-sensitive workloads. These edge sites are connected to the central data centers, creating a distributed computing topology where workloads are placed at the optimal point on the latency-bandwidth spectrum.

4. Sustainability and Energy Efficiency

The power demands of AI and large-scale computing are enormous, and the environmental impact of data centers has become a critical concern. The industry is moving toward energy-efficient networking components such as Linear Pluggable Optics (LPO), which reduce power consumption in optical transceivers. Advanced cooling solutions, including liquid cooling and immersion cooling (where servers are submerged in a thermally conductive but electrically insulating fluid), are replacing traditional air cooling, which is increasingly inadequate for the heat densities of modern GPU clusters. Sustainability is no longer a secondary concern; it is a primary design constraint.

5. Enhanced Security and Visibility

With increased reliance on data center uptime comes an intensified focus on network observability, security, and resilience. Traditional monitoring tools are insufficient for the scale and complexity of modern fabrics. AIOps (Artificial Intelligence for IT Operations) uses machine learning to analyze network telemetry, detect anomalies, perform predictive maintenance, and identify potential security breaches before they cause outages. The ability to “see” everything happening in the network in real time, and to respond automatically, is becoming a fundamental requirement.

Clarifications

Most Commonly Confused Points

VLAN vs. Subnet: VLAN = Layer 2 broadcast domain isolation. Subnet = Layer 3 IP address range. They usually map 1:1 but are conceptually different.

MPLS replaces IP? NO. MPLS coexists with IP. The IP header is preserved inside the labeled packet. MPLS provides an alternative forwarding mechanism (label-based) but does not eliminate IP.

Trunk port vs. Access port: Access = one VLAN, untagged, connects to hosts. Trunk = multiple VLANs, 802.1Q tagged, connects between switches.

VXLAN vs. VLAN: VLAN = 12-bit ID, L2 only, single campus. VXLAN = 24-bit VNID, L2-over-L3 tunneling, crosses data centers.

RDMA vs. RoCE: RDMA = Remote Direct Memory Access (the memory-access technique). RoCE = RDMA over Converged Ethernet (the protocol that carries RDMA over Ethernet). RoCE is one way to implement RDMA; InfiniBand is another.

Viva Voce

Q: Why can't we just use subnets instead of VLANs for isolation?
Subnets operate at Layer 3 and control IP routing, but they do not prevent Layer 2 broadcast traffic from reaching all hosts on the same physical switch. A broadcast frame (like an ARP request) is flooded to all ports regardless of subnet. VLANs provide Layer 2 isolation, containing broadcasts within their virtual LAN. Both are needed: VLANs for L2 isolation, subnets for L3 addressing.
Q: What happens if an 802.1Q-tagged frame arrives at a non-VLAN-aware switch?
The switch will see the EtherType field as 0x8100, which it does not recognize. Standard behavior for unrecognized EtherTypes is to drop the frame. This is why 802.1Q tags are only placed on trunk ports connecting VLAN-aware switches, and removed before frames reach access ports and end hosts.
Q: How does MPLS differ from IP routing in terms of forwarding decision?
IP routing makes forwarding decisions based on the destination IP address using longest prefix matching. MPLS makes forwarding decisions based on a fixed-length label using exact match, which is faster. More importantly, IP routing is destination-based only, while MPLS can make forwarding decisions based on both source and destination, enabling traffic engineering.
Q: Why does a data center use a leaf-spine topology instead of a traditional three-tier tree?
A traditional tree creates bottleneck links at the aggregation and core layers (oversubscription) and has only one path between any two servers (single point of failure). Leaf-spine provides multiple equal-cost paths (higher throughput and redundancy), predictable 2-hop latency between any two racks, and scales horizontally by adding more spine switches.
Q: What is the difference between RDMA and RoCE?
RDMA (Remote Direct Memory Access) is a technique for directly accessing a remote machine's memory without OS kernel involvement. RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet) is a specific protocol that implements RDMA over standard Ethernet networks. RDMA is the capability; RoCE is one way to deliver it (InfiniBand is another).
Q: Why does the professor ask "Are protocols dying?" in the context of SDN?
With SDN (like Google ORION), functions traditionally performed by distributed protocols—routing, traffic engineering, failover—are centralized in a software controller. Switches become simple forwarding devices programmed by the controller. Traditional distributed protocols like OSPF are replaced by controller applications. The protocols aren't literally dying, but their role is shifting from distributed decision-making to simple data-plane programming interfaces.
Q: Why does VXLAN use a 24-bit VNID instead of the 12-bit VLAN ID?
The 12-bit VLAN ID supports only 4,096 VLANs, which is insufficient for large cloud providers who need to isolate traffic for thousands or millions of tenants. The 24-bit VNID supports approximately 16.7 million virtual networks, providing the scale needed for multi-tenant cloud environments.
Q: What is the purpose of the Priority Code Point (PCP) field in 802.1Q?
The 3-bit PCP field allows Ethernet frames to be assigned one of eight priority levels (0-7) for Quality of Service (QoS) processing. Higher-priority frames (like voice traffic) can be given preferential treatment in switch queues, ensuring lower latency and jitter. It functions similarly to the IP ToS/DSCP field but operates at Layer 2.
