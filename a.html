Section 2.1

The Imperative of Requirement Analysis

Imagine commissioning a house. You tell the architect you want something comfortable, with enough rooms, and good light. The architect nods, draws plans, and six months later you walk into a structure where the kitchen opens into the bathroom and the only window faces a brick wall. The house satisfies every word you said — it has rooms, it has light, it is technically habitable — and yet it is profoundly wrong. This is the catastrophe that requirement analysis exists to prevent. In software engineering, the gap between what users imagine they need and what developers believe they heard is not a minor inconvenience; it is the primary source of project failure, budget overruns, and systems that function perfectly according to specification while serving no useful purpose.

Requirement analysis is the systematic process of discovering, documenting, and validating what a software system must do, how it must behave, and what constraints it must satisfy. It is not a single conversation with a client. It is not a form that stakeholders fill out. It is a disciplined engineering activity that involves elicitation (drawing out needs from people who often cannot articulate them), analysis (resolving contradictions and filling gaps), specification (writing precise, testable statements), and validation (confirming that what was written actually matches what was meant). The Standish Group's famous CHAOS reports consistently identify poor requirements as the leading cause of software project failure — not coding errors, not inadequate testing, not slow hardware, but the simple, devastating failure to understand what was supposed to be built in the first place.

Requirement Analysis is the systematic process of gathering, interpreting, documenting, and validating the needs and constraints of stakeholders to produce a clear, complete, and consistent specification of what a software system must accomplish. It bridges the world of human needs and the world of technical implementation.

The importance of this phase can be understood through a simple economic argument. An error discovered during requirements costs approximately one unit to fix. The same error discovered during design costs five units. During coding, ten. During testing, twenty. After deployment, two hundred. This exponential cost curve — well-documented by Barry Boehm and others — means that a misunderstood requirement is not merely an inconvenience; it is a financial time bomb. Getting requirements right is not about perfectionism; it is about survival.

Three distinct concepts must be disentangled at the outset. User needs are the raw, often unstructured desires and problems that stakeholders express — "I need a way to track inventory" or "The current system is too slow." Software features are specific capabilities the software will provide to address those needs — "real-time stock dashboard with low-stock alerts." Software requirements are precise, verifiable statements that the system must satisfy — "The system shall display current inventory levels for all SKUs within 2 seconds of a query, and shall generate an alert when any SKU falls below its reorder threshold." The journey from needs to features to requirements is the journey from vagueness to precision, and it is the core intellectual labor of this phase.

A requirement is not what the user wants. It is what the system must do to satisfy what the user needs — expressed precisely enough that both the user and the engineer can agree on whether it has been met.
Section 2.2

Requirements Engineering: The Complete Process

Requirements engineering is the overarching discipline that encompasses every activity related to discovering, representing, and managing requirements throughout the software lifecycle. It is not a single phase that concludes before design begins; rather, it is a continuous process that evolves as understanding deepens and circumstances change. However, for the purpose of structured study and examination, it is typically described through four sub-phases that unfold sequentially (with inevitable iteration): elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation.

Figure 1 — The four sub-phases of Requirements Engineering with iterative feedback loop

Elicitation

Elicitation is the process of gathering requirements from stakeholders, existing documents, and operational environments. The word "elicitation" is deliberately chosen over "collection" — requirements are rarely lying around waiting to be picked up. They must be drawn out through interviews, workshops, observation, questionnaires, prototyping, and study of existing systems. Stakeholders often do not know what they need, or they express wants that conflict with their actual work patterns, or they assume certain behaviors are obvious when they are not. The elicitor must be part detective, part therapist, and part anthropologist. Common techniques include structured and unstructured interviews, joint application development (JAD) sessions, ethnographic observation, document analysis, and prototyping. Each technique has strengths: interviews reveal individual perspectives; JAD sessions expose conflicts between stakeholders; observation uncovers implicit work practices that users never mention because they are too obvious to them; prototyping gives stakeholders something concrete to react to, which often clarifies their needs far more effectively than abstract discussion.

Analysis

Raw requirements gathered during elicitation are inevitably incomplete, contradictory, ambiguous, and unstructured. Analysis is the process of making sense of this material: resolving conflicts between stakeholders (the marketing department wants a feature that the security team forbids), identifying missing requirements (nobody mentioned error handling, but the system must have it), classifying requirements into categories (functional, non-functional, constraints), and modeling the requirements using formal or semi-formal representations. Analysis also involves prioritization — determining which requirements are essential, which are desirable, and which are aspirational. This is often a politically charged activity because every stakeholder believes their requirements are the most critical, and the analyst must mediate these claims against constraints of budget, schedule, and technical feasibility.

Specification

Specification is the act of writing the analyzed requirements into a formal, structured document — the Software Requirements Specification (SRS). This document becomes the contract between the development team and the stakeholders, the reference against which the final system will be tested, and the foundation for all subsequent design and implementation decisions. The SRS must be precise enough to be testable, complete enough to leave no significant gaps, and understandable enough that both technical and non-technical readers can verify its accuracy. Writing a good SRS is one of the most intellectually demanding activities in software engineering because it requires translating human intent into unambiguous technical language without losing the nuance of the original need.

Validation

Validation answers the question: "Did we write the right requirements?" It is not about whether the requirements are well-formed (that is a quality concern addressed during specification) but about whether they accurately reflect stakeholder needs. Validation activities include requirements reviews (systematic reading and checking by peers and stakeholders), prototyping (building partial implementations to test whether requirements produce the expected behavior), model validation (checking that analytical models accurately represent the problem domain), and acceptance test definition (writing tests that the final system must pass, which forces clarity about what "done" means). Validation typically reveals errors and omissions that feed back into elicitation and analysis, which is why the overall process is iterative rather than strictly linear.

Why Requirements Engineering Is the Most Critical Phase

No other phase has as large an impact on the final system. Design errors affect architecture; coding errors affect components; testing errors affect quality assurance. Requirement errors affect everything. A system built to the wrong specification is wrong at every level, no matter how elegant its architecture or how thorough its testing. Furthermore, requirement errors are the hardest to detect because they appear to be correct — the system does exactly what the specification says, which is exactly the wrong thing. The later a requirement error is found, the more of the system must be reworked, which is why the cost-of-change curve rises so steeply. This is why requirements engineering is often called the most critical phase: it sets the trajectory for everything that follows, and a wrong trajectory cannot be corrected by running faster.

Section 2.3

Classes of Requirements: Enduring & Volatile

Not all requirements are created equal in their stability. Some describe fundamental properties of the problem domain that will remain true for the lifetime of the system — a bank will always need to maintain accurate account balances, a hospital will always need to protect patient confidentiality. Others describe properties that may change as the business environment evolves, as regulations shift, or as organizational strategies pivot. Understanding this distinction is crucial because it determines how requirements should be documented, how rigidly they should be enforced, and how much architectural flexibility should be preserved to accommodate future change.

Enduring requirements are those that derive from the fundamental nature of the problem domain and remain stable throughout the system's lifetime. Volatile requirements are those that are likely to change during the system's lifetime due to environmental, organizational, or technological shifts.

Enduring requirements are the bedrock. For a library management system, the requirement that every book must be catalogued and traceable to its current location is enduring — it flows from the very concept of what a library is. Volatile requirements, by contrast, are the weather. For the same library system, the specific loan period (14 days, 21 days, 30 days), the fine structure, and the digital format policies are all subject to change as the library's policies evolve. Somerville classifies volatile requirements further into mutable requirements (requirements that change naturally over time), emergent requirements (requirements that arise as the system is used and stakeholders understand it better), consequential requirements (requirements that change as a consequence of introducing the system itself — the system changes the work environment, which changes the requirements), and compatibility requirements (requirements that change because the system must coexist with other evolving systems).

Exam strategy: When asked about enduring vs volatile requirements, always give domain examples. A requirement like "the system shall process financial transactions accurately" is enduring; "the system shall support GST at 18%" is volatile — tax rates change.
Section 2.4

Functional vs Non-Functional Requirements

This distinction is among the most examined concepts in software engineering and also among the most misunderstood. Functional requirements describe what the system does — the specific behaviors, operations, and information processing that the system must perform. Non-functional requirements describe how the system behaves — the quality attributes, constraints, and performance characteristics that govern the system's operation. If functional requirements are the verbs of a system, non-functional requirements are the adverbs — they do not change what is done, but they profoundly change how well it is done.

Functional requirements specify the functions that a system must perform — the computations, data transformations, and interactions with the external environment. Non-functional requirements specify the quality attributes, performance bounds, and constraints that the system must satisfy, such as response time, security, reliability, and portability.

A functional requirement for an ATM would state: "The system shall allow a customer to withdraw cash from a validated account." A non-functional requirement for the same system would state: "The system shall complete a withdrawal transaction within 10 seconds of receiving valid input." Both are essential. A system that allows withdrawal but takes 45 minutes per transaction technically satisfies the functional requirement but fails the non-functional one — and is effectively useless. Conversely, a system that responds in 2 seconds but miscalculates balances satisfies the non-functional requirement while catastrophically failing the functional one. Neither type is more important than the other; they address different dimensions of quality.

Non-functional requirements are further classified into categories: performance (response time, throughput), reliability (mean time between failures, fault tolerance), security (authentication, authorization, encryption), usability (learnability, accessibility), portability (platform independence), maintainability (modularity, documentation), and efficiency (resource utilization). A critical point for exams: non-functional requirements are often more difficult to specify precisely than functional requirements because they tend to be qualitative ("the system should be fast") rather than quantitative ("the system shall respond within 3 seconds"). The engineering discipline of requirements work lies in converting the former into the latter.

Memory Hook: Functional = What it does (verbs). Non-Functional = How well it does it (adverbs). Think: "The car drives" (functional) vs "The car drives at 120 km/h with 5-star safety" (non-functional).
Section 2.5

User Requirements vs System Requirements

The requirements of a software system exist at different levels of abstraction, and conflating these levels is a reliable source of confusion and error. User requirements are high-level statements of what the system must do, written in the language of the problem domain and intended for stakeholder consumption. They describe the services the system will provide and the constraints under which it will operate, expressed in natural language alongside diagrams and simple tables, comprehensible to non-technical readers. System requirements are detailed, precise descriptions of what the system must implement, written in the language of the solution domain and intended for the development team. They expand user requirements into specific functions, interfaces, data structures, and behaviors that engineers can design and code against.

Consider a hospital management system. A user requirement might state: "The system shall allow doctors to retrieve a patient's complete medical history." This is clear, meaningful, and verifiable by the doctor. The corresponding system requirements would specify the exact query mechanisms, the database schema for medical records, the network protocols for retrieval, the display format, the response time bounds, the access control rules, and the error handling for missing records. User requirements answer the question "what should the system do for its users?"; system requirements answer the question "what specifically must the system implement to deliver those capabilities?"

Section 2.6

Barriers to Eliciting User Requirements

If elicitation were simply a matter of asking people what they need, requirements engineering would be trivial. In practice, it is extraordinarily difficult, and the reasons for that difficulty are not merely technical but deeply human. Understanding these barriers is essential because each one demands specific strategies to overcome, and a requirements engineer who is unaware of them will produce specifications that look complete but are riddled with gaps and errors.

First, stakeholders often do not know what they need. This is not stupidity; it is a fundamental property of expertise. People who have performed a task the same way for years often cannot articulate what they do because their knowledge has become tacit — embedded in muscle memory and judgment rather than in conscious rules. A seasoned nurse may know intuitively when a patient's vital signs are concerning without being able to specify the exact thresholds or patterns that trigger her concern. When asked to describe her requirements for a monitoring system, she will describe what she thinks she does, not what she actually does, and the gap between the two can be vast.

Second, stakeholders express what they want rather than what they need. A user might request a specific feature — "I need a button that exports to Excel" — when the underlying need is "I need to share data with my team in a format they can use." The specific feature request may be suboptimal; perhaps a shared dashboard or a CSV export would serve the need better. The elicitor must dig beneath requests to understand the underlying problem.

Third, political and organizational dynamics distort requirements. Different stakeholders have different interests, and these interests may conflict. The finance department wants strict cost controls; the marketing department wants rapid feature releases. A department head may inflate requirements to increase budget or influence. Stakeholders may withhold information to protect their territory. These dynamics cannot be resolved by technical means; they require negotiation, mediation, and sometimes executive arbitration.

Fourth, there is the communication gap between technical and non-technical people. Engineers think in terms of data structures, algorithms, and interfaces. Users think in terms of tasks, outcomes, and frustrations. When an engineer asks "What are your functional requirements?" and the user replies "I just need it to work," both parties are speaking in good faith but in fundamentally different languages. The elicitor must translate between these worlds without losing information in either direction.

Additional barriers include the volatility of the business environment (requirements change between the time they are elicited and the time the system is delivered), the difficulty of specifying non-functional requirements precisely, the "yes, but" syndrome where stakeholders accept a specification and then reject the implemented system because seeing the real thing reveals needs they could not articulate beforehand, and the sheer scale of complex systems where no single stakeholder understands the entire problem domain.

Strategies to Overcome Barriers
  • For tacit knowledge: Use ethnographic observation — watch users work rather than asking them to describe it. Prototyping helps users react to concrete artifacts rather than abstract descriptions.
  • For want-vs-need confusion: Use root-cause analysis and "five whys" technique to trace feature requests back to underlying problems.
  • For political dynamics: Use facilitated workshops (JAD sessions) where conflicting stakeholders must negotiate in real time. Document priorities explicitly.
  • For communication gaps: Use multiple representation formats — natural language, diagrams, prototypes, scenarios — so stakeholders can engage with whichever format makes sense to them.
  • For volatility: Design for change. Identify volatile requirements explicitly and architect the system to accommodate their modification without structural rework.
Section 2.7

The SRS Document & Black-Box Specification

The Software Requirements Specification is the single most important document produced during the requirements phase. It is simultaneously a communication vehicle between stakeholders and developers, a contract between the client and the builder, and the benchmark against which the completed system will be validated. A well-written SRS concentrates in one place everything that the system must be, must do, and must not do, and it does so in a manner precise enough to be tested and complete enough to be the sole reference for design.

The SRS (Software Requirements Specification) is a formal document that completely describes the external behavior of the proposed system, without specifying its internal implementation. It defines what the system must do, the constraints under which it must operate, and the criteria by which it will be accepted.

The SRS is often called a black-box specification because it describes the system's externally observable behavior — its inputs, outputs, and responses — without revealing anything about the internal structure that produces that behavior. The SRS treats the system as a black box: it specifies what goes in, what comes out, and what constraints govern the transformation, but it does not specify how the transformation is accomplished. This is a deliberate design choice. By separating what the system does from how it does it, the SRS gives the design team maximum freedom to choose the best implementation approach. If the SRS prescribed internal algorithms or data structures, it would prematurely constrain the design, potentially preventing better solutions. The black-box principle ensures that the SRS is a specification of the problem, not of a particular solution.

Characteristics of a Good SRS

An SRS that lacks quality can be worse than no SRS at all because it creates a false sense of agreement. The IEEE 830 standard identifies the following qualities that every SRS must possess. These are exam staples and must be understood, not merely listed:

  • Correctness: Every requirement stated in the SRS must accurately represent something required of the system by a stakeholder. If the SRS says the system shall send confirmation emails but the stakeholders never requested this, the SRS is incorrect.
  • Unambiguity: Every requirement must have exactly one interpretation. "The system shall respond quickly" is ambiguous; "the system shall respond within 3 seconds" is unambiguous.
  • Completeness: The SRS must contain all significant requirements — nothing important should be missing. If the SRS does not mention error handling, it is incomplete.
  • Consistency: No two requirements should contradict each other. If one requirement says "all data shall be encrypted" and another says "log files shall be human-readable," and the log files contain data, there is a contradiction.
  • Verifiability: Every requirement must be testable — there must be a finite, cost-effective process to determine whether the system satisfies it. "The system shall be user-friendly" is not verifiable; "80% of new users shall complete a registration task within 5 minutes without assistance" is.
  • Traceability: Every requirement must be traceable to its source (why does this requirement exist?) and forward to design, code, and test elements (where is this requirement implemented and tested?).
  • Modifiability: The SRS must be structured so that changes can be made easily and consistently without disrupting the overall document. This requires good organization, cross-referencing, and version control.

Structure of an SRS Document

While various standards exist (IEEE 830 being the most widely referenced), a typical SRS contains the following sections:

SectionContents
1. IntroductionPurpose, scope, definitions, references, overview
2. General DescriptionProduct perspective, functions, user characteristics, constraints, assumptions

Example SRS Outline: Project Management Software

1. Introduction — Purpose: define requirements for a web-based project management tool. Scope: task tracking, team collaboration, reporting. Definitions: Sprint, Kanban, Burndown chart. References: IEEE 830-1998.

NFR-02: System shall maintain 99.5% uptime measured monthly. Interface: REST API for third-party integrations.

4. Appendices — Data models, wireframe mockups, glossary.

Section 2.8

Feasibility Study

Before committing resources to a full-scale requirements analysis and development effort, the organization must determine whether the proposed system is even viable. The feasibility study is a preliminary investigation that assesses whether a project is worth pursuing, conducted before significant investment of time and money. It is not a detailed technical analysis; it is a rapid, high-level assessment that answers the fundamental question: "Should we proceed?"

A feasibility study typically examines four dimensions. Technical feasibility asks whether the technology exists to build the system — can the required performance, reliability, and functionality be achieved with available tools and expertise? Economic feasibility asks whether the benefits of the system justify its costs — will the financial returns, cost savings, or strategic advantages outweigh the development and operational expenses? Operational feasibility asks whether the system will actually be used — will the intended users accept it, will it fit into existing workflows, and will it solve the problem it is intended to solve? Schedule feasibility asks whether the system can be delivered within the required timeframe. A system that is technically possible, economically justified, and operationally viable but cannot be delivered in time for its intended purpose is not feasible.

Exam note: A feasibility study is conducted before detailed requirements analysis. It uses approximate estimates, not precise calculations. Its purpose is to prevent the organization from investing in projects that are doomed from the start.
Section 2.9

Tools for Requirements Gathering: Decision Tables & Decision Trees

When a system's behavior depends on complex combinations of conditions — if condition A is true and condition B is false and condition C is true, then perform action X, but if A and B are both true regardless of C, then perform action Y — natural language descriptions become tangled and error-prone. Decision tables and decision trees are two complementary tools for representing such conditional logic with precision and clarity. Both serve the same fundamental purpose — to map combinations of conditions to the actions that should result — but they do so in different visual formats that suit different cognitive styles and different types of complexity.

Decision Tables

A decision table is a tabular representation that systematically lists all possible combinations of conditions and the corresponding actions. It is divided into four quadrants: the condition stub (listing all conditions), the condition entries (showing the truth values of each condition for each rule), the action stub (listing all possible actions), and the action entries (indicating which actions are performed for each rule). Each column in the table represents one decision rule — a specific combination of conditions and the actions that result. The power of a decision table lies in its completeness: by systematically enumerating all combinations, it ensures that no possibility is overlooked, and it reveals contradictions and gaps in the logic that would be invisible in a prose description.

Decision Trees

A decision tree represents the same logical structure as a graph where internal nodes represent conditions (decision points), branches represent possible values of those conditions, and leaf nodes represent the actions to be taken. The tree is read from root to leaf: at each decision point, follow the branch corresponding to the current condition's value until you reach an action. Decision trees are more visually intuitive than decision tables for people who think in terms of sequential decision-making, and they are particularly effective when the logic has a natural hierarchical structure — the most important conditions are tested first, narrowing the possibilities progressively.

Document flowcharts, another traditional tool, represent the movement of documents through an organization — how a purchase order flows from request to approval to procurement to payment. While largely superseded by DFDs for system modeling, they remain useful for understanding the administrative context in which a software system operates, particularly in organizations with complex paper-based procedures.

Section 2.10

Structured Analysis: DFDs & Data Dictionary

Structured analysis is the methodological framework that dominated requirements modeling from the late 1970s through the 1990s and remains deeply embedded in university curricula and examination patterns. Its core proposition is elegant: a system can be understood by modeling the flow of data through it — where data originates, how it is transformed, where it is stored, and where it exits. The two primary artifacts of structured analysis are the Data Flow Diagram (DFD), which provides the visual model, and the Data Dictionary, which provides the precise definitions.

Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs)

A Data Flow Diagram (DFD) is a graphical representation of the flow of data through a system, showing how input data is transformed into output through processes, and how data is stored and moved between processes, external entities, and data stores. DFDs model what the system does with data, not how it does it.

DFDs use four fundamental symbols (in the Gane-Sarson notation most commonly taught in Indian universities): External entities (rectangles) represent sources or destinations of data outside the system — people, organizations, or other systems. Processes (rounded rectangles or circles) represent transformations of data — every process takes input data and produces output. Data stores (open-ended rectangles or double lines) represent repositories of data at rest — files, databases, or any persistent storage. Data flows (arrows) represent the movement of data between the other three elements, always labeled with the name of the data being moved.

Figure 3 — The four fundamental DFD symbols (Gane-Sarson notation)

DFDs are drawn in levels of increasing detail, a technique called leveling or decomposition. The Context Diagram (Level 0) shows the entire system as a single process with all external entities and data flows crossing the system boundary. It establishes scope: what is inside the system and what is outside. The Level 1 DFD decomposes the single process from the context diagram into its major subprocesses, showing how data flows between them and to/from data stores. Each subprocess can be further decomposed into a Level 2 DFD, and so on, until processes are simple enough to be understood without further decomposition. The critical rule of decomposition is that every data flow entering or leaving a process at one level must correspond to data flows entering or leaving the decomposed version at the next level — this is balancing, and it ensures consistency between levels.

Figure 4 — Context Diagram (Level 0 DFD) for Safe Home Security System

The context diagram above shows the Safe Home system as a single process interacting with four external entities. The homeowner sends passwords and configuration commands into the system and receives alerts and status displays in return. Sensors send intrusion and fire detection signals. The system sends alarm calls to the police and fire department, and event logs to a monitoring company. Notice what the diagram does not show: how the system validates the password, how it decides whether to call the police or the fire department, how event data is formatted, or what database stores the configuration. All of that detail is deferred to lower-level DFDs. The context diagram answers one question only: what crosses the boundary of the system?

Level 1 DFD: Employee Management System

Decomposing the single "Employee Management System" process from a context diagram reveals the major subsystems. At Level 1, we typically identify 3–7 processes that represent the core functional areas:

  • P1 — Manage Employee Records: Receives employee data from HR, validates it, stores in D1 (Employee DB). Outputs employee records to reporting processes.
  • P2 — Process Attendance: Receives attendance data (swipe logs) from employees, validates against schedules in D2 (Attendance DB), outputs attendance records.
  • P3 — Calculate Payroll: Receives attendance records from P2 and salary structure from D1, computes pay, outputs salary slips to employees and payroll reports to finance.
  • P4 — Generate Reports: Receives queries from management, pulls data from D1 and D2, outputs statistical and compliance reports.

Each data flow must balance with the context diagram: the external entities and their data flows remain identical, but now we see the internal processes, data stores, and internal data flows that were hidden in the single bubble of Level 0. If the context diagram shows "Employee data" flowing from HR into the system, then the Level 1 DFD must show that same "Employee data" flowing from HR into one of the internal processes — it cannot disappear or transform without a process to transform it.

The Data Dictionary

A DFD without a data dictionary is like a map without a legend — the shapes are visible but their meaning is ambiguous. The data dictionary provides precise, formal definitions of every data flow, data store, and data element referenced in the DFDs. It eliminates the ambiguity that arises when different people interpret the same term differently. Does "customer information" include the shipping address or just the billing address? Does "order total" include tax? The data dictionary answers these questions definitively.

A Data Dictionary is a structured repository that defines the composition, structure, and meaning of all data elements, data flows, data stores, and processes in a system. It serves as the authoritative reference for understanding every data item in the DFD models.

Each entry in the data dictionary typically includes the name of the data item, its alias (other names by which it is known), a description of its purpose, its composition (the sub-elements it contains), its data type and length, and any validation rules. For composite data items, the dictionary uses a simple notation: + denotes sequence (composed of), [ | ] denotes selection (one of the listed alternatives), { } denotes repetition (one or more occurrences), and ( ) denotes optional elements.

Data Dictionary Example Entries

Order = Order_ID + Customer_Info + {Order_Line} + Order_Total + Order_Date

Customer_Info = Customer_ID + Name + [Billing_Address | Shipping_Address] + (Email)

Order_Line = Product_ID + Quantity + Unit_Price + Line_Total

Order_Total = Sum(Line_Total) + Tax

The notation "+" means "and" (sequence), "{ }" means "one or more", "[ | ]" means "one of", and "( )" means "optional".

The data dictionary and the DFDs are interdependent: the DFDs show where data flows, and the dictionary defines what that data is. Together, they form a complete model of the system's information architecture. Every arrow on every DFD must have a corresponding entry in the data dictionary, and every data dictionary entry must appear on at least one DFD. This mutual traceability ensures consistency and completeness.

Section 2.11

Non-Traditional Requirements Analysis Tools: FSM, Statecharts & Petri Nets

Structured analysis with DFDs excels at modeling the flow of data through a system, but it has a significant blind spot: it does not naturally represent the behavior of a system over time — the sequence of states the system passes through, the events that trigger transitions between states, and the conditions that must hold for a transition to occur. For systems where behavior is the central concern — real-time systems, embedded controllers, communication protocols, and interactive user interfaces — behavioral modeling tools provide representations that DFDs cannot. The three principal behavioral modeling tools in the requirements analyst's repertoire are Finite State Machines, Statecharts, and Petri Nets.

Finite State Machines (FSM)

A Finite State Machine is the simplest and most fundamental behavioral model. It represents a system as a finite set of states, with transitions between states triggered by events. At any given moment, the system is in exactly one state. When an event occurs, the system transitions to a new state (which may be the same state if the event does not warrant a change). Every FSM has an initial state (where the system begins) and may have one or more final states (where the system terminates). The complete behavior of the system is defined by the set of all possible states and the transitions between them.

A Finite State Machine (FSM) is a mathematical model of computation consisting of a finite set of states, a set of input events, a transition function that maps each (state, event) pair to a new state, an initial state, and a set of final states. It models system behavior as a sequence of state transitions triggered by discrete events.
Figure 5 — Finite State Machine model for ATM transaction behavior

The FSM's strength is its simplicity and formal rigor — every state and transition is explicitly enumerated, leaving no ambiguity about system behavior. Its weakness is state explosion: for complex systems, the number of states grows combinatorially. A system with 10 independent two-state variables has 2¹⁰ = 1024 states, most of which are combinations that the system never actually enters. This practical limitation motivated the development of more sophisticated behavioral models.

Statecharts

Statecharts, invented by David Harel in 1987, extend finite state machines with three powerful mechanisms that directly address the state explosion problem. Hierarchical nesting allows states to contain substates, so a complex state like "Operating" can be decomposed into "Initializing," "Running," and "Shutting Down" without requiring each substate to be separately connected to every external transition. Orthogonality (concurrency) allows the system to be in multiple independent states simultaneously — the system can be in "Heating" state for the temperature controller and "Rotating" state for the motor controller at the same time, without needing a combined "HeatingAndRotating" state. Broadcast communication allows transitions in one part of the system to trigger transitions in another, enabling the modeling of complex event interactions.

In a statechart, a superstate encloses its substates within a rounded rectangle. Transitions entering or leaving the superstate apply to all substates — for example, a "power-off" event causes a transition from any substate of "Operating" to the "Off" state, without needing separate transitions from each substate. Orthogonal regions are drawn as dashed compartments within a state, each containing an independent state machine that operates concurrently. This compact notation can represent systems with thousands of effective states in a diagram of manageable size, making statecharts the preferred notation for real-time and embedded system specification.

Petri Nets

Petri nets, introduced by Carl Adam Petri in 1962, model concurrent, asynchronous, distributed systems with an explicit representation of both states and events. Unlike FSMs, where the system's state is an atomic element, Petri nets decompose state into distributed places (represented as circles) that may hold tokens (representing resources, conditions, or data), and transitions (represented as bars or rectangles) that represent events or actions. A transition fires (executes) when all its input places contain tokens — this is the enabling condition. When a transition fires, it consumes one token from each input place and produces one token in each output place. The distribution of tokens across all places at any given moment is called the marking and represents the current state of the system.

A Petri Net is a bipartite directed graph consisting of places (circles), transitions (bars), directed arcs connecting places to transitions and transitions to places, and tokens (dots in places) that represent the current state. It models concurrent systems by representing both conditions (places with tokens) and events (transitions that fire when enabled).
Figure 6 — Petri Net for sequential order processing (token in "Order Received" place)

The real power of Petri nets emerges in modeling concurrency and synchronization. A transition with multiple input places fires only when all its input places have tokens — this models synchronization (waiting for multiple conditions to be simultaneously true). A transition with multiple output places produces tokens in all output places simultaneously — this models parallelism (forking into concurrent activities). Two transitions that share input tokens but only one can fire model conflict or choice. These properties make Petri nets uniquely suited for modeling distributed systems, workflow processes, and communication protocols where multiple activities proceed in parallel and must occasionally coordinate.

Case Study: SRS for a Real-Time System

Real-time systems demand a particularly rigorous approach to requirements specification because the consequences of failure are severe — a missed deadline in a patient monitoring system is not an inconvenience but a potential fatality. To demonstrate how the concepts of this unit converge in practice, consider the SRS for a Real-Time Patient Monitoring System (RTPMS) — a system that continuously collects vital signs from hospitalized patients, alerts nursing staff when parameters exceed safe thresholds, and maintains historical records for clinical review.

SRS Outline: Real-Time Patient Monitoring System

1. Introduction

1.1 Purpose: This SRS defines the requirements for a real-time patient monitoring system that continuously tracks vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, SpO₂, temperature, respiration rate) for up to 64 patients per nursing station, generating immediate alerts for critical conditions.

1.2 Scope: The system covers data acquisition from bedside sensors, real-time display at the nursing station, threshold-based alerting, historical data storage, and clinical reporting. It does not cover sensor hardware maintenance or diagnostic interpretation by physicians.

1.3 Definitions: SpO₂ = peripheral capillary oxygen saturation; EWS = Early Warning Score; Sampling interval = time between consecutive sensor readings.

2. General Description

2.1 Product Perspective: The RTPMS interfaces with bedside physiological sensors via wired connections, displays real-time data on nursing station monitors, and forwards alerts to mobile devices. It is a self-contained system with interfaces to the hospital's Electronic Health Record (EHR) system for data exchange.

2.2 User Characteristics: Primary users are trained nurses (comfortable with medical equipment, variable computer literacy). Secondary users are physicians (review trends, adjust thresholds). System administrators configure and maintain the system.

2.3 Constraints: C1: System must operate on hospital intranet (no external internet dependency). C2: Must comply with medical device regulations (ISO 13485). C3: Data retention for minimum 7 years. C4: Real-time response — no human intervention between sensor reading and display.

2.4 Assumptions: A1: Bedside sensors are calibrated and transmit data in standard HL7 format. A2: Nursing station has uninterruptible power supply. A3: Maximum 64 patients per station.

3. Specific Requirements

3.1 Functional Requirements:

  • FR-01: The system shall acquire vital sign data from each connected sensor at a sampling interval of 1 second ± 50ms.
  • FR-02: The system shall display real-time vital signs for all monitored patients on the nursing station screen, updating within 2 seconds of data acquisition.
  • FR-03: The system shall generate an audible and visual alert within 3 seconds of detecting that any vital sign exceeds its configured threshold.
  • FR-04: The system shall allow authorized nurses to acknowledge alerts, silencing the audible alarm while maintaining the visual indicator.
  • FR-05: The system shall store all vital sign readings with timestamps in the patient data store.
  • FR-06: The system shall compute and display Early Warning Scores (EWS) based on current vital signs, updated every 30 seconds.
  • FR-07: The system shall allow physicians to customize alert thresholds per patient.
  • FR-08: The system shall export historical patient data to the EHR system in HL7 FHIR format.

3.2 Non-Functional Requirements:

  • NFR-01 (Performance): The system shall process and display data from all 64 patients with end-to-end latency not exceeding 2 seconds under maximum load.
  • NFR-02 (Reliability): The system shall have a Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) of at least 10,000 hours. Failure of one patient's sensor interface shall not affect monitoring of other patients.
  • NFR-03 (Safety): The system shall fail-safe — in case of system failure, it shall generate a continuous audible alarm indicating loss of monitoring capability.
  • NFR-04 (Security): Access to patient data shall require authentication. Threshold modifications shall be logged with user identity and timestamp.
  • NFR-05 (Availability): The system shall maintain 99.99% uptime (maximum 52.6 minutes downtime per year).

3.3 Interface Requirements:

  • IF-01: Sensor Interface — RS-485 serial, HL7 data format, 1-second polling interval.
  • IF-02: Display Interface — 1920×1080 minimum resolution, support for 64-patient overview and single-patient detail views.
  • IF-03: EHR Interface — RESTful API, HL7 FHIR R4 resources, HTTPS transport.
  • IF-04: Alert Interface — Mobile push notification via hospital WiFi, SNMP trap to central monitoring.

This case study illustrates several principles that distinguish real-time system requirements from conventional information system requirements. The timing constraints (latency not exceeding 2 seconds, alerts within 3 seconds) are not merely desirable qualities — they are hard requirements whose violation constitutes system failure. The fail-safe requirement (NFR-03) is characteristic of safety-critical systems: when the system cannot perform its function, it must enter a state that is maximally safe, which in this case means alarming loudly rather than silently failing. The independence requirement (failure of one patient's interface does not affect others) drives architectural decisions toward redundancy and isolation that would be unnecessary in a non-real-time system.

Master Revision & Exam Arsenal

The following revision cards, memory hooks, and exam-question frameworks are designed for rapid pre-exam review. Each card compresses a major topic into its irreducible core — the minimum you must recall to reconstruct a full 10-mark answer under exam pressure. The key to using these effectively is not to memorize them as rigid scripts but to internalize the conceptual structure so that the details flow naturally from the framework during the exam.

Rapid Revision: Requirements Engineering Process

  • Elicitation → gathering needs (interviews, JAD, observation, prototyping, document analysis)
  • Analysis → resolving conflicts, classifying, prioritizing, modeling
  • Specification → writing the SRS (formal, structured, testable)
  • Validation → confirming "did we write the right requirements?" (reviews, prototyping, acceptance tests)
  • The process is iterative — validation feeds back into elicitation and analysis
  • Most critical phase because requirement errors are the costliest to fix (exponential cost curve)
Memory Hook: Think "EASV" — Elicit, Analyze, Specify, Validate. Or: "Engineers Always Study Vigilantly" — the RE process is about studying what to build with maximum vigilance.

Rapid Revision: Functional vs Non-Functional Requirements

  • Functional = WHAT the system does (behaviors, computations, transformations)
  • Non-functional = HOW WELL it does it (performance, security, reliability, usability, portability)
  • NFR categories: Performance, Reliability, Security, Usability, Portability, Maintainability, Efficiency
  • NFRs must be quantified ("respond within 3s" not "respond quickly")
  • Both are essential — functional correctness without non-functional quality = unusable system
  • Functional = verbs; Non-functional = adverbs

Rapid Revision: SRS Document

  • SRS = black-box specification (external behavior only, no internal design)
  • IEEE 830 qualities: Correct, Unambiguous, Complete, Consistent, Verifiable, Traceable, Modifiable
  • Structure: Introduction → General Description → Specific Requirements → Appendices
  • Black-box: specifies inputs, outputs, constraints — NOT algorithms or data structures
  • Purpose: communication vehicle, contract, validation benchmark
Memory Hook for SRS qualities: "CUCC VTM" — Correct, Unambiguous, Complete, Consistent, Verifiable, Traceable, Modifiable. Imagine a vintage CUCC-VTM television set — a quirky but memorable anchor.

Rapid Revision: DFDs & Data Dictionary

  • 4 symbols: External entity (rectangle), Process (rounded rect), Data store (open rect), Data flow (arrow)
  • Leveling: Context (Level 0) → Level 1 (major processes) → Level 2 (sub-processes) → ...
  • Balancing: every data flow at one level must appear at the next level of decomposition
  • Data Dictionary: defines every data item — composition, type, validation rules
  • DD notation: + (sequence), [ | ] (selection), { } (repetition), ( ) (optional)
  • DFDs show WHERE data flows; DD shows WHAT the data is — they are interdependent

Rapid Revision: FSM, Statecharts, Petri Nets

  • FSM: states + events + transitions. Simple but suffers state explosion.
  • Statecharts (Harel): FSM + hierarchy + orthogonality + broadcast. Solves state explosion.
  • Petri Nets: places (circles) + tokens + transitions (bars). Models concurrency and synchronization.
  • Transition fires when all input places have tokens (enabling condition).
  • FSM = sequential; Statecharts = complex reactive; Petri Nets = concurrent/distributed

Rapid Revision: Decision Tables & Trees

  • Decision Table: condition stub, condition entries, action stub, action entries. Each column = one rule.
  • Decision Tree: internal nodes = conditions, branches = values, leaves = actions.
  • Tables ensure completeness (all combinations enumerated). Trees are more intuitive for sequential logic.
  • Tables scale poorly with many conditions (2ⁿ rules). Trees handle sequential decisions better.

Answer Expansion Strategy: How to Write a Full 10-Mark Answer

The most common reason students score poorly on 10-mark questions is not lack of knowledge but lack of structured expansion. A 10-mark answer must demonstrate breadth, depth, and organization. Use the following framework to expand any concept into a complete answer:

  1. Introduction (2 marks): Define the concept formally. State its significance. Give a one-sentence intuition before the technical definition.
  2. Core Explanation (4 marks): Elaborate the concept in detail. Explain the mechanism step-by-step. Use diagrams wherever possible (and label them). Give examples from real systems.
  3. Comparison / Classification (2 marks): Classify the concept into subtypes or compare it with related concepts. Use tables for comparison questions. This demonstrates analytical thinking beyond mere description.
  4. Advantages & Disadvantages (1 mark): List practical benefits and limitations. This shows mature understanding, not just textbook recall.
  5. Conclusion (1 mark): Briefly state the concept's importance in the broader context of software engineering. Connect to downstream phases (design, testing, maintenance).
Model Answer Expansion: "Explain Requirement Engineering in detail" (10 marks)

Introduction: Requirements Engineering (RE) is the systematic process of discovering, documenting, and maintaining the set of requirements that a software system must satisfy. It is widely regarded as the most critical phase because errors at this stage propagate exponentially through all subsequent phases — Boehm's cost-of-change curve shows that a requirement error found after deployment costs 200× more to fix than one found during elicitation.

Core — Four Sub-phases:

(1) Elicitation: Gathering requirements from stakeholders using techniques like interviews, JAD sessions, ethnographic observation, prototyping, and document analysis. The key challenge is that users often cannot articulate what they need — they express wants rather than needs, and their tacit knowledge is difficult to externalize.

(2) Analysis: Processing raw elicited requirements to resolve conflicts, identify gaps, classify requirements (functional/non-functional, enduring/volatile), and prioritize them. Analysis often reveals that different stakeholders have contradictory requirements that must be negotiated.

(3) Specification: Writing the analyzed requirements into the SRS document — a formal, structured, testable specification of what the system must do. The SRS is a black-box specification that describes external behavior without prescribing internal design.

(4) Validation: Confirming that the SRS accurately reflects stakeholder needs through reviews, prototyping, and acceptance test definition. Validation typically feeds back into elicitation and analysis, making RE iterative.

Why Most Critical: The exponential cost curve; requirement errors affect the entire system; a correctly implemented wrong requirement is worse than a bug because it appears correct; no amount of good design or testing can compensate for wrong requirements.

Conclusion: RE sets the trajectory for the entire project. Getting it right is not about perfectionism but about avoiding the compounding costs of fundamental misunderstandings. Modern agile approaches integrate RE as a continuous activity rather than a front-loaded phase, but the four sub-phases remain conceptually essential.

Model Answer Expansion: "Draw DFD for Online Railway Reservation (IRCTC)" (10 marks)

Introduction: A Data Flow Diagram models the flow of data through a system using four symbols — external entities, processes, data stores, and data flows. It shows what the system does with data without specifying how. The IRCTC railway reservation system handles ticket booking, cancellation, and enquiry for millions of passengers.

Level 0 (Context Diagram): The entire system is a single process "IRCTC Railway Reservation System" with external entities: Passenger (sends search queries, booking requests, payment, cancellation requests; receives tickets, refunds, status), Railway Admin (sends train schedule data, fare updates; receives booking reports), and Payment Gateway (receives payment requests; sends payment confirmation).

Level 1 DFD: Decompose into major processes:

  • P1 — Train Enquiry: Receives search criteria from Passenger, queries D1 (Train Schedule DB), returns availability and fare information.
  • P2 — Ticket Booking: Receives booking request from Passenger, checks availability via D1, creates reservation record in D2 (Reservation DB), sends payment request to Payment Gateway, receives confirmation, generates ticket (PNR) sent to Passenger.
  • P3 — Cancellation: Receives cancellation request from Passenger, retrieves booking from D2, calculates refund, updates D2, initiates refund via Payment Gateway, sends cancellation confirmation to Passenger.
  • P4 — Schedule Management: Receives schedule updates from Railway Admin, updates D1.
  • P5 — Report Generation: Queries D2 and D1, sends booking and revenue reports to Railway Admin.

Balancing Check: Every data flow crossing the system boundary in Level 0 appears identically in Level 1 — Passenger still sends search queries, booking requests, etc., and receives tickets, status, etc. No external data flows are added or removed.

Booking_Request = Train_Number + Journey_Date + Source_Station + Destination_Station + Passenger_Details + Class_Code

Passenger_Details = {Name + Age + [Male|Female|Other] + (ID_Proof_Type + ID_Number)}

Ticket = PNR_Number + Train_Number + Journey_Date + Source_Station + Destination_Station + Class_Code + {Passenger_Info} + Total_Fare + Booking_Status

Common Mistakes in Exams:
  1. Confusing functional and non-functional requirements. Security is non-functional, not functional. "The system shall require login" is functional; "login shall use AES-256 encryption" is non-functional.
  2. Drawing unbalanced DFDs. A data flow that appears at Level 0 must appear at Level 1 with the same name and connected to the same external entity. Students frequently drop or rename flows between levels.
  3. Showing control flow in DFDs. DFDs model data flow, not control flow. Do not draw arrows for "go to step 2" or "trigger process B." Only data — named, meaningful information — flows on DFD arrows.
  4. Calling SRS a design document. The SRS specifies WHAT the system does, not HOW. Including algorithms, database schemas, or class diagrams in an SRS violates the black-box principle.
  5. Listing non-quantifiable non-functional requirements. "The system shall be fast" earns no marks. "The system shall respond within 3 seconds for 95% of queries" does.
  6. Confusing user requirements with system requirements. User requirements are high-level, in natural language, for stakeholders. System requirements are detailed, technical, for developers. They exist at different abstraction levels.
  7. Treating FSM and Petri Nets as interchangeable. FSMs model sequential state transitions. Petri Nets model concurrent, distributed behavior with explicit synchronization. They solve different problems.
Master Memory Framework — The "RE House":
Imagine a house. The foundation is Elicitation (gathering the raw materials). The walls are Analysis (structuring and resolving conflicts). The roof is Specification (the SRS document that covers and protects). The inspector is Validation (checking everything is built correctly). The door is the SRS — a black-box: you can see what goes in and comes out, but not the internal structure. The furniture is Functional Requirements (what you can do inside). The building code is Non-Functional Requirements (how safe, warm, and durable it must be). The weather is Volatile Requirements (things that change from outside). The bedrock is Enduring Requirements (things that never change).
