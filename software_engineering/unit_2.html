I Part One

The Foundation: Why Requirements Matter

Before a single line of code is written, before an architecture is sketched on a whiteboard, before a database table is named, there is a moment of reckoning in every software project: the moment when someone must articulate what the software is actually supposed to do. This deceptively simple act—stating what we want a system to accomplish—turns out to be one of the most error-prone, consequential, and intellectually demanding activities in all of software engineering. The discipline that studies this act is called Requirement Engineering, and its importance cannot be overstated: studies from the Standish Group, IBM Systems Sciences Institute, and Barry Boehm's seminal work all converge on the same uncomfortable finding that defects originating in the requirements phase are not merely common—they are the most expensive class of defects to fix, with costs escalating by a factor of 50 to 200 times if discovered after deployment compared to when they are caught during the requirements phase itself.

Consider what happens when requirements go wrong. The Therac-25 radiation therapy machine, perhaps the most cited example in software engineering ethics, delivered lethal doses of radiation partly because the software requirements did not adequately account for race conditions in the operator interface. The system was designed to prevent certain unsafe conditions, but the requirements specification did not anticipate that operators might rapidly re-enter commands faster than the system could process safety interlocks. This was not a coding error—it was a requirements failure, a failure to ask the right questions about how the system would actually be used under time pressure by human operators. The consequences were fatal. Closer to everyday experience, the initial launch of the Healthcare.gov website in the United States in 2013 was a catastrophe partly rooted in requirements issues: multiple contractors built different parts of the system against ambiguous, overlapping, and sometimes contradictory requirements, and no single entity had a coherent, testable specification of what the integrated system was supposed to do.

The single most expensive mistake in software development is building the wrong thing correctly. Requirements engineering exists to prevent precisely this tragedy.

The importance of requirement analysis can be understood along several dimensions, each of which provides independent justification for treating the requirements phase with extraordinary care. First, there is the cost dimension: Boehm's curve shows that the cost of fixing an error grows exponentially as it is discovered later in the lifecycle. A misunderstood requirement that takes one hour to correct during elicitation might take one hundred hours to correct during integration testing and one thousand hours to correct after deployment—assuming it is even correctable without replacing the entire architectural foundation. Second, there is the scope dimension: requirements define the boundary of the project. Without clear requirements, there is no reliable way to determine when the project is complete, whether a particular feature belongs in the current release, or whether a change request represents an enhancement or a correction. Third, there is the communication dimension: the requirements document serves as a contract between the client and the development team, and between different members of the development team. It is the single artifact that all stakeholders can point to when disagreements arise about what was promised, what was intended, and what was delivered. Fourth, there is the quality dimension: every subsequent phase of development—design, coding, testing, deployment—is constrained by the quality of the requirements. Testing, in particular, is only as effective as the requirements against which test cases are derived. If a requirement is ambiguous or missing, no test case will check for that condition, and the defect will sail through testing undetected.

Historical Motivation

In the early decades of software development (1960s–1970s), requirements were often implicit—developers built what they thought the user needed, sometimes without consulting the user at all. The "waterfall" model, formalized by Winston Royce in 1970, was itself a reaction to the chaos of unstructured development, and it placed requirements as the first explicit phase. The discipline of Requirement Engineering emerged in the 1990s as researchers recognized that requirements were not merely a phase to be "gotten through" but a complex, iterative, sociotechnical activity requiring its own methods, tools, and theoretical foundations.

For examination purposes, when you are asked "Why is requirement engineering considered the most critical phase of software development?", you should structure your answer around these pillars: cost escalation (with Boehm's curve as evidence), scope definition, stakeholder communication, quality foundation, and risk mitigation. Each pillar can be expanded into a paragraph, and you should include at least one concrete example (such as Therac-25 or Healthcare.gov) to demonstrate that you understand the real-world consequences, not just the theoretical arguments. A ten-mark answer on this question should devote roughly two marks to each pillar, with an introductory paragraph that frames the importance and a concluding paragraph that connects back to the overall software development lifecycle.

II Part Two

Anatomy of Requirements: Types and Classifications

The word "requirement" is deceptively uniform. In practice, requirements form a layered hierarchy, ranging from vague human needs to precise technical specifications, and each layer serves a different audience, serves a different purpose, and demands a different notation. Understanding these layers is essential because confusing them—or collapsing them into a single undifferentiated list—is one of the most common sources of specification errors in practice. A user need is not a software feature, and a software feature is not a system requirement, though they are all related and they all constrain what the final system must do. Let us unpack each of these concepts carefully.

User Needs

A user need is a statement of what the user wants to accomplish, expressed in the user's own vocabulary, without any assumption about how the system will satisfy it. User needs are typically discovered through interviews, observation, questionnaires, and workshops with the people who will actually use the system. They are written in natural language and they describe goals, problems, and desires rather than solutions. For example, "I need to be able to find out whether a book is available in the library without physically going to the library" is a user need. Notice that it says nothing about web interfaces, databases, search algorithms, or network protocols—it is purely a statement of desire from the user's perspective. User needs are the raw material from which all subsequent requirements are derived, and they are the ultimate test of whether the final system is successful: if the system does not satisfy user needs, it has failed regardless of how elegant its architecture or how efficient its algorithms.

Software Features

A software feature is a identifiable unit of functionality that the system will provide to satisfy one or more user needs. Features are at a higher level of abstraction than detailed requirements but at a lower level than user needs—they represent concrete capabilities that can be described, demonstrated, and tested. If the user need is "find out whether a book is available without going to the library," then a corresponding software feature might be "online catalog search" or "real-time availability checker." Features are useful for project planning because they can be prioritized, estimated, and scheduled. They are also useful for communication with non-technical stakeholders because they describe what the system does in terms that can be demonstrated and discussed without requiring technical knowledge. However, features are not yet requirements in the formal sense because they lack the precision needed for design and implementation.

Software Requirements

A software requirement is a precise, verifiable statement of a condition or capability that the system must possess. Requirements are derived from features but add the specificity needed for design, implementation, and testing. Where a feature says "online catalog search," a requirement says "The system shall allow users to search the catalog by title, author, ISBN, or keyword, and shall return results within 3 seconds for catalogs containing up to 1 million entries." Notice the addition of specific search fields (title, author, ISBN, keyword) and a measurable performance criterion (3 seconds for up to 1 million entries). This specificity is what distinguishes a requirement from a feature: requirements are testable, traceable, and unambiguous. If you can write a test case that definitively determines whether the requirement is met, it is a well-formed requirement; if you cannot, it is still a feature or a need that needs further refinement.

Common Student Confusion: Needs vs. Features vs. Requirements

Students frequently conflate these three concepts, especially in exam answers. The key distinction is the level of abstraction and verifiability: needs are abstract and unverifiable (you cannot test "convenience"), features are moderately specific and demonstrable, and requirements are precise and testable. Think of it as a funnel: needs are wide and fuzzy at the top, requirements are narrow and sharp at the bottom. In an exam, if you are asked to distinguish between them, always provide one running example (like the library system above) and show how the same desire transforms through all three levels.

Enduring and Volatile Requirements

Not all requirements are created equal in their stability over time. Some requirements reflect fundamental properties of the problem domain and will remain true regardless of how the technology, the organization, or the market changes. Others are contingent on current circumstances and may change even during the development of the project. This distinction, first systematically articulated by Ian Sommerville, classifies requirements into two categories: enduring requirements and volatile requirements.

Enduring requirements are those that derive from the core activity of the organization and are stable over the lifetime of the system. They are rooted in the fundamental nature of the problem domain. For a banking system, the requirement that "every financial transaction must be recorded with a timestamp, amount, and account identifier" is enduring because it reflects the fundamental nature of financial accounting, which has not changed for centuries. For a hospital management system, the requirement that "patient records must be maintained with complete medical history" is enduring because it derives from the nature of medical practice itself. Enduring requirements form the stable backbone of the system architecture and should be identified early because they constrain the most fundamental design decisions.

Volatile requirements are those that are likely to change during the system's lifetime. They may change because of new business rules, new regulations, new technology, or evolving user expectations. Volatile requirements can be further subclassified into several types that are worth knowing for exam purposes. Mutable requirements are requirements that change because of the customer's environment—for example, a tax calculation module must be updated whenever the government changes tax rates. Emergent requirements are requirements that cannot be fully defined until the system is partially operational and users can react to it—for example, users of a new workflow management system may not know exactly what reporting features they need until they have used the system for several months. Consequential requirements are requirements that arise as a consequence of introducing the new system—for example, introducing an online ordering system may create a new requirement for a customer-facing FAQ page that did not exist before. Compatibility requirements are requirements that arise from the need to interoperate with other systems or comply with external standards—for example, a new hospital system must comply with HL7 standards for health data exchange, and these standards themselves evolve over time.

Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements

This is perhaps the most fundamental classification of requirements and the one most frequently tested in examinations. A functional requirement describes what the system should do—its behaviors, its operations, its responses to inputs. A non-functional requirement describes how well the system should do it—its quality attributes, its constraints, its performance characteristics. The distinction is not merely academic; it has profound implications for architecture, testing, and project management.

Functional requirements specify the services the system must provide, how it must react to particular inputs, and what it must produce as output. They are expressed as statements of the form "The system shall [do something]." Examples include: "The system shall allow a registered user to reset their password via email verification," "The system shall calculate the total order amount including applicable taxes and discounts," and "The system shall generate a monthly sales report sorted by region." Functional requirements are typically derived directly from user needs and features, and they are the primary input to use case modeling, sequence diagram creation, and test case design. They define the "what" of the system.

Non-functional requirements, on the other hand, specify the quality attributes, constraints, and performance expectations of the system. They are not about what the system does but about the manner in which it does it. The standard classification includes several categories that you should know by name: performance requirements (response time, throughput, capacity—"The system shall handle 500 concurrent users with average response time under 2 seconds"), reliability requirements (mean time between failures, availability—"The system shall have 99.9% uptime"), security requirements (authentication, authorization, encryption—"All user passwords shall be stored using bcrypt with a minimum work factor of 12"), usability requirements (learnability, accessibility—"A new user shall be able to complete the registration process within 3 minutes without external help"), portability requirements (platform independence, browser compatibility—"The system shall function on Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge without feature degradation"), and maintainability requirements (modularity, documentation—"The system shall be designed so that any single module can be replaced without affecting more than two other modules").

User Requirements vs. System Requirements

The distinction between user requirements and system requirements is primarily one of audience and abstraction level, though the two are deeply interrelated. User requirements are statements of what the system must do from the perspective of the end user, written in natural language with minimal technical jargon, often using diagrams and scenarios to illustrate usage patterns. They are intended to be read and validated by non-technical stakeholders—customers, managers, end users themselves. System requirements, by contrast, are more detailed, more precise, and more technical. They describe what the system must implement in order to satisfy the user requirements, and they may include specifications of interfaces, data structures, algorithms, and protocols that are invisible to the end user but essential for the developer. System requirements are the bridge between the user's world and the developer's world, and they must be precise enough to serve as the basis for design and testing.

Consider the difference for a university registration system. A user requirement might state: "Students shall be able to register for courses online during the registration period." A corresponding system requirement might state: "The system shall provide a web-based interface accessible via HTTPS that allows authenticated students with a valid registration window to add or drop courses, subject to prerequisite checking, seat availability, and maximum credit limits, and shall persist all registration changes to the database within a single ACID-compliant transaction." The user requirement captures the intent; the system requirement specifies the implementation constraints that make the intent achievable.

III Part Three

The Requirement Engineering Process

Requirement Engineering (RE) is not a single activity but a structured collection of sub-activities that together transform vague human needs into a precise, validated, and agreed-upon specification. The standard decomposition, as described in most textbooks following Sommerville and Kotonya, identifies four or five major sub-phases. For Indian university examinations, the most commonly accepted decomposition includes the following sub-phases: Feasibility Study, Requirements Elicitation, Requirements Analysis, Requirements Specification, and Requirements Validation. Some authors also include Requirements Management as a separate ongoing phase. Let us examine each in detail.

Figure 1 — The Requirement Engineering Process with Iterative Feedback Loop

1. Feasibility Study

Before investing significant resources in detailed requirements engineering, a feasibility study determines whether the proposed system is worthwhile pursuing at all. This is a preliminary investigation that examines three dimensions of feasibility. Technical feasibility asks whether the technology exists (or can be developed within the project's constraints) to build the system. For example, if a client wants a real-time image recognition system that processes 4K video at 60fps on a smartphone, the feasibility study must determine whether current mobile processors and machine learning frameworks can achieve this. Economic feasibility (also called cost-benefit analysis) asks whether the system will provide sufficient return on investment to justify its development and maintenance costs. This involves estimating both tangible benefits (reduced labor costs, increased revenue) and intangible benefits (improved customer satisfaction, better decision-making). Operational feasibility asks whether the system, once built, will actually be used effectively within the organization's existing processes, culture, and skill base. A brilliant system that the staff refuses to use because it disrupts their workflow is operationally infeasible. Additional dimensions sometimes considered include legal feasibility (does the system comply with applicable laws and regulations?) and schedule feasibility (can the system be delivered within the required timeframe?).

2. Requirements Elicitation

Requirements elicitation is the process of gathering requirements from stakeholders through various techniques. This is not merely "collecting" requirements as if they already exist fully formed in the stakeholders' minds—rather, it is a process of discovery, negotiation, and co-creation. The word "elicitation" is deliberately chosen over "collection" because it implies that requirements often need to be drawn out, sometimes from stakeholders who cannot articulate them clearly. Techniques for elicitation include: interviews (structured, semi-structured, or unstructured conversations with stakeholders), questionnaires and surveys (useful when a large number of stakeholders need to be consulted), observation (watching users perform their current tasks to understand implicit requirements), document analysis (examining existing forms, reports, manuals, and procedures), prototyping (building a partial or simulated version of the system to elicit feedback), joint application development (JAD) sessions (structured workshops where stakeholders and developers work together), brainstorming (free-form group discussion to generate ideas), and ethnographic studies (extended immersion in the user's work environment). Each technique has its strengths and weaknesses, and in practice, a combination of techniques is always used because no single technique can uncover all types of requirements.

3. Requirements Analysis

Once requirements have been elicited, they must be analyzed for completeness, consistency, ambiguity, and feasibility. Requirements analysis involves classifying requirements into functional and non-functional categories, identifying conflicts between requirements (e.g., one stakeholder wants real-time updates while another wants minimal network traffic), resolving conflicts through negotiation and prioritization, detecting missing requirements through systematic cross-checking, and modeling the requirements using appropriate notations (DFDs, use cases, state diagrams). Analysis also involves identifying requirement problems such as incompleteness (missing information), inconsistency (contradictions between requirements), ambiguity (multiple possible interpretations), unfeasibility (requirements that cannot be implemented within constraints), and volatility (requirements likely to change).

4. Requirements Specification

Requirements specification is the process of documenting the analyzed requirements in a formal document—the Software Requirements Specification (SRS) document. This document serves as the authoritative reference for all subsequent development activities and as the contractual basis between the client and the development team. We will examine the SRS document in detail in Part V.

5. Requirements Validation

Requirements validation is the process of checking that the documented requirements actually define the system that the customer wants. It is distinct from verification (which checks whether the system meets its specification) in that validation checks whether the specification itself is correct. Techniques for validation include: requirements reviews (formal or informal inspections of the SRS document by stakeholders and peers), prototyping (building executable models to demonstrate requirements), test case generation (deriving test cases from requirements—if a test case cannot be written, the requirement is ambiguous), traceability matrices (ensuring every requirement traces back to a user need and forward to a design element), and scenario analysis (walking through usage scenarios to check whether requirements cover all cases). Errors found during validation are fed back to the elicitation and analysis phases, forming the iterative loop shown in the diagram above.

IV Part Four

Barriers to Eliciting User Requirements

The elicitation of requirements is perhaps the most human-dependent activity in software engineering, and wherever humans are deeply involved, things become messy. The barriers to effective requirements elicitation are not primarily technical—they are cognitive, organizational, and social. Understanding these barriers is essential for the exam and for practice, because recognizing a barrier is the first step toward designing a strategy to overcome it. The following are the most commonly cited barriers, each explained with its underlying mechanism and potential mitigation strategies.

1. Cognitive barriers: Users don't know what they want. This is perhaps the most fundamental barrier. Many users have never thought systematically about what they need from a system; they know their current pain points but cannot articulate what an ideal solution would look like. This is not a failure of intelligence—it is a cognitive limitation. People are generally better at recognizing whether a proposed solution meets their needs than at generating a specification of those needs from scratch. Mitigation: use prototyping, scenario-based elicitation, and observation rather than relying solely on direct questioning. Show users something concrete and let them react to it.

2. Expression barriers: Users know what they want but cannot express it clearly. Even when users have a clear mental model of their needs, they may lack the vocabulary, the analytical framework, or the confidence to express those needs in terms that engineers can use. Users often describe their needs in domain-specific jargon that engineers do not understand, or they describe needs at the wrong level of abstraction—too vague for engineers to act on, or too implementation-specific for engineers to evaluate. Mitigation: employ domain experts as translators, use structured interviews with prepared questions, and employ visual modeling tools (use cases, storyboards) that reduce reliance on verbal expression alone.

3. Organizational and political barriers. In many organizations, different stakeholders have conflicting interests. The marketing department wants a feature-rich product that can be demonstrated at trade shows; the operations department wants a stable, maintainable system with minimal complexity; the finance department wants the cheapest possible solution. These conflicts are not merely misunderstandings—they reflect genuine differences in organizational power, incentives, and priorities. Stakeholders may deliberately withhold information, inflate their requirements, or misrepresent their needs to gain advantage in the political process. Mitigation: use structured negotiation techniques, involve a neutral facilitator in requirements workshops, and document all stakeholder positions explicitly so that tradeoffs are visible and decisions are traceable.

4. Volatility barriers: Requirements change during the project. Even after requirements have been carefully elicited and documented, they may change because the business environment changes, because stakeholders learn more about what they need, or because new regulations are imposed. This is particularly problematic in traditional plan-driven methodologies where changes after the requirements phase are expensive. Mitigation: adopt iterative development methodologies that accommodate change, design the architecture to be flexible (especially isolating volatile requirements), and establish a formal change management process.

5. Communication barriers between developers and users. Developers and users often inhabit different conceptual worlds. Developers think in terms of data structures, algorithms, and interfaces; users think in terms of tasks, goals, and outcomes. This conceptual gap leads to mutual misunderstanding even when both parties are communicating in good faith. A user says "the system should be fast" and means "I shouldn't have to wait while doing my daily work"; a developer interprets it as "response time should be under 500 milliseconds"—which may or may not be the same thing depending on the context. Mitigation: use concrete examples and scenarios rather than abstract descriptions, involve users in prototype evaluation, and maintain a glossary of terms that both parties agree on.

6. The "Yes, but" syndrome. This occurs when users review a completed system (or prototype) and say "Yes, but..." followed by a fundamental change in direction that should have been identified much earlier. The syndrome reflects the fact that people often cannot evaluate a specification abstractly but can easily identify what is wrong with a concrete implementation. Mitigation: deliver working software incrementally rather than in a single big-bang release, so that the "yes, but" reactions occur early when changes are still inexpensive.

Exam Strategy: "Discuss barriers to eliciting user requirements"

For a 10-mark answer: (1) Define what elicitation means and why barriers exist (1 mark), (2) List and explain 5-6 barriers with examples (6 marks), (3) For each barrier, mention at least one mitigation strategy (2 marks), (4) Conclude by connecting barriers to the need for iterative RE processes (1 mark). Always use real-world examples—even hypothetical ones—to ground each barrier.

V Part Five

The SRS Document: Structure, Standards, and Craft

The Software Requirements Specification (SRS) document is the single most important artifact produced during the requirements engineering process. It is simultaneously a communication medium, a contractual document, a validation benchmark, and a foundation for all subsequent development. Getting the SRS right is not merely a matter of following a template—it is an act of technical writing that requires precision, completeness, consistency, and a deep understanding of both the problem domain and the development process. The SRS is sometimes called a "black-box specification" because it describes what the system must do (its external behavior and interfaces) without prescribing how it must do it (its internal design and implementation). This black-box property is crucial: the SRS should describe the problem, not the solution, because premature design decisions embedded in the SRS constrain the design phase unnecessarily and may lead to suboptimal architectures.

Why is the SRS called a "Black-Box" Specification?

The term "black box" is borrowed from systems engineering and refers to a system whose internal workings are unknown or irrelevant; only its inputs, outputs, and external behavior are specified. When we say the SRS is a black-box specification, we mean that it specifies the system's functionality from the outside—what inputs it accepts, what outputs it produces, what constraints it must satisfy—without specifying the internal architecture, algorithms, data structures, or implementation technology. This separation of "what" from "how" is fundamental because it allows the design team to explore multiple implementation approaches and choose the best one, rather than being locked into a particular solution by the requirements document. If an SRS says "The system shall store customer records in a relational database using third normal form," it has crossed the line from black-box specification into design—it has prescribed a solution rather than a requirement. A proper black-box SRS would instead say "The system shall store customer records such that no data redundancy exists and all records can be retrieved by customer ID within 1 second."

Characteristics of a Good SRS

The IEEE 830 standard (and its successor, ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148) identifies several quality characteristics that every SRS should possess. These characteristics are not merely aspirational—they are measurable criteria against which the quality of an SRS can be evaluated, and they are frequently tested in examinations.

IEEE 830 Quality Characteristics
  • Correctness: Every requirement stated in the SRS must accurately represent something that is required of the system. A correct SRS is one that, if implemented exactly, produces a system that satisfies all stakeholder needs. Correctness can only be fully verified by the stakeholders themselves through review.
  • Unambiguous: Every requirement must have one and only one interpretation. Ambiguity often arises from natural language—words like "fast," "reliable," "user-friendly," and "adequate" mean different things to different people. The SRS must either avoid such terms entirely or define them quantitatively within the document.
  • Completeness: The SRS must define all significant requirements relating to functionality, performance, design constraints, attributes, and external interfaces. A complete SRS leaves no questions unanswered—if a reader has to guess about any aspect of the system's behavior, the SRS is incomplete.
  • Consistency: No requirement in the SRS may conflict with any other requirement. Consistency must hold across all dimensions: logical consistency (no contradictions), terminological consistency (terms used with the same meaning throughout), and behavioral consistency (the system's behavior is coherent across all specified conditions).
  • Verifiability: Every requirement must be testable—that is, there must exist a finite, cost-effective process to determine whether the implemented system satisfies the requirement. "The system shall be easy to use" is unverifiable; "A first-time user shall be able to complete registration within 3 minutes without assistance" is verifiable.
  • Modifiability: The SRS must be structured so that changes can be made easily, consistently, and completely. This requires a well-organized document with a table of contents, cross-referencing, and modular structure.
  • Traceability: Every requirement must be traceable backward to its source (a user need, a business rule, a regulation) and forward to its implementation (design element, code module, test case). Traceability ensures that no requirement is orphaned and that every design decision is justified by a requirement.

Structure of the SRS Document

The IEEE 830 standard recommends a specific structure for the SRS document. While real-world SRS documents may vary, the IEEE structure is the standard against which exam answers are evaluated. The following is the recommended outline:

IEEE 830 SRS Document Structure
  • 1. Introduction
    • 1.1 Purpose of the document
    • 1.2 Scope of the product
    • 1.3 Definitions, acronyms, and abbreviations
    • 1.4 References
    • 1.5 Overview of the document
  • 2. General Description
    • 2.1 Product perspective (context, interfaces)
    • 2.2 Product functions (summary of major functions)
    • 2.3 User characteristics
    • 2.4 Constraints
    • 2.5 Assumptions and dependencies
  • 3. Specific Requirements

    • 3.6 Other requirements
  • 4. Appendices
  • 5. Index

Each section of the SRS serves a specific purpose and requires careful attention. The Introduction sets the stage by explaining why the document exists, what it covers, and what terms and references the reader needs to know. The General Description provides a high-level overview of the product without going into detail—its purpose is to give readers a mental model of the system before they dive into specific requirements. The Specific Requirements section is the heart of the document and typically occupies 70–80% of the total content. It must contain every functional requirement, every non-functional requirement, every interface specification, and every constraint in sufficient detail for design and testing. The Appendices contain supporting material such as data formats, message protocols, or sample calculations that would clutter the main document.

Case Study: SRS for a Real-Time System

To ground these abstract concepts, let us consider a simplified SRS for a Real-Time Patient Monitoring System—a system that continuously monitors patients' vital signs in a hospital intensive care unit and raises alarms when parameters exceed safe thresholds. This example is chosen because real-time systems have particularly demanding non-functional requirements (timing, reliability, safety) that illustrate the full scope of an SRS.

SRS Excerpt: Real-Time Patient Monitoring System

1. Introduction

1.1 Purpose: This document specifies the requirements for the Real-Time Patient Monitoring System (RPMS), which continuously monitors vital signs of ICU patients and alerts medical staff to life-threatening conditions.

1.2 Scope: RPMS shall monitor heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and body temperature for up to 64 patients simultaneously. It shall provide visual and auditory alarms, trend displays, and data logging.

2. General Description

2.1 Product Perspective: RPMS interfaces with bedside sensors, a central monitoring station, nurse call systems, and the hospital information system (HIS).

2.3 User Characteristics: Primary users are ICU nurses (medical training, limited computer expertise) and physicians. System must be operable during high-stress situations.

2.4 Constraints: Must comply with IEC 60601-1-8 (medical alarm systems) and HIPAA (patient data privacy).

3. Specific Requirements (selected)

3.2 Functional Requirements:

  • FR-01: The system shall acquire vital sign data from each connected sensor at intervals no greater than 1 second.
  • FR-02: The system shall compare each vital sign reading against configurable upper and lower thresholds for that patient.
  • FR-03: When a vital sign reading exceeds a threshold, the system shall generate a visual alarm on the central monitoring station and an auditory alarm at the bedside within 2 seconds of the reading being received.
  • FR-04: The system shall log all vital sign readings and all alarm events with timestamps to a persistent database.

3.3 Performance Requirements:

  • NFR-P01: The system shall process and display vital sign data with a maximum latency of 1 second from sensor reading to screen display under normal load (64 patients).
  • NFR-P02: The system shall have an availability of at least 99.99% (maximum downtime of approximately 52 minutes per year).

3.5 Attributes:

  • NFR-S01 (Safety): A single hardware or software failure shall not cause the loss of more than one patient's monitoring capability. The system shall employ redundant processing.
  • NFR-S02 (Security): All patient data transmitted over the network shall be encrypted using AES-256.

Notice how each requirement in this case study is numbered (for traceability), is written in a "shall" statement (for verifiability), and specifies measurable criteria (for testability). The real-time constraints—"within 2 seconds," "maximum latency of 1 second," "99.99% availability"—are typical of real-time systems and illustrate how non-functional requirements can be just as critical as functional requirements. A patient monitoring system that functions correctly but generates alarms 30 seconds after a crisis is, for all practical purposes, a failed system, even though it technically "meets" the functional requirement of generating alarms. This is why non-functional requirements must be specified with the same precision as functional requirements—they are not optional embellishments; they are defining characteristics of the system.

VI Part Six

Tools for Requirements Gathering: Document Flow Charts, Decision Tables, Decision Trees

Once requirements have been elicited from stakeholders, they must be organized, analyzed, and represented in forms that support reasoning, communication, and validation. Natural language, while indispensable for expressing requirements in human-readable form, is inherently ambiguous, verbose, and difficult to analyze for completeness and consistency. Supplementary notations—visual and tabular—address these limitations by providing structured representations that make logic explicit, reveal gaps, and enable systematic analysis. Three such notations are the Document Flow Chart, the Decision Table, and the Decision Tree, each suited to a different type of requirement reasoning.

Document Flow Chart

A document flow chart (also called a document flowchart or paperwork flowchart) is a specialized type of flowchart that traces the flow of documents, forms, reports, and data through an organization or a system. Unlike a standard program flowchart that shows the flow of control in a program, a document flow chart shows the flow of information—where documents originate, how they are processed, who handles them, where copies are filed, and what triggers the next step. Document flow charts are particularly useful during requirements analysis because they reveal the existing information flow in the organization, identify bottlenecks and redundancies, and help the analyst understand how the proposed system must integrate with existing business processes.

The standard symbols used in document flow charts include: rectangles for processes or operations, parallelograms for documents or reports, small circles for connectors, diamonds for decision points, and arrows for the flow direction. Each document is typically labeled with its name and the number of copies produced at each stage. The chart is read from top to bottom or from left to right, following the arrows, and it provides a complete picture of the document lifecycle from creation to archival.

Decision Table

A decision table is a tabular representation of complex business rules that maps combinations of conditions to the actions that should be taken. When a business rule involves multiple conditions, each of which can be true or false, the number of possible combinations grows exponentially, and natural-language descriptions of such rules are notoriously error-prone. Decision tables solve this problem by organizing all conditions and actions into a structured grid where every possible combination is explicitly accounted for.

The structure of a decision table has four quadrants. The upper-left quadrant lists the conditions (the decision variables). The upper-right quadrant contains the condition entries—the possible values (typically Y for yes/true, N for no/false, or - for irrelevant) for each condition in each rule. The lower-left quadrant lists the actions that can be taken. The lower-right quadrant contains the action entries—typically X marks indicating which actions should be taken for each combination of conditions. Each column in the right half of the table represents one rule.

Figure 2 — Decision Table: Discount Rules for Order Processing

Decision Tree

A decision tree is a graphical representation of the same logic that a decision table captures, but in a tree-structured format that many people find more intuitive. Each internal node of the tree represents a condition (a decision variable), each branch represents a possible value of that condition, and each leaf node represents an action or outcome. The tree is read from the root (the first condition tested) down through successive branches to a leaf (the action taken). Decision trees are particularly useful when the number of conditions is small and the logic is sequential (i.e., which condition is tested next depends on the outcome of the previous test), because the tree structure makes the sequential testing order visually explicit.

VII Part Seven

Structured Analysis: DFDs and Data Dictionaries

Structured analysis, developed in the late 1970s by Tom DeMarco, Larry Constantine, and others, is one of the most enduring and widely taught methods for analyzing and specifying requirements. At its core, structured analysis treats a system as a transformation engine that converts inputs into outputs through a series of processes, with data flowing between processes, being stored in data stores, and entering or leaving the system through external entities. The two primary tools of structured analysis are the Data Flow Diagram (DFD) and the Data Dictionary, which together provide a complete picture of the system's functional decomposition and data definitions.

Data Flow Diagrams (DFD)

A Data Flow Diagram is a graphical representation of the flow of data through a system. It shows the processes that transform data, the data stores that hold data, the external entities that interact with the system, and the data flows that connect these elements. DFDs are intentionally abstract—they do not show control flow, timing, or sequence of operations; they show only what data moves where. This abstraction is a feature, not a limitation, because it allows the analyst to focus on the functional decomposition of the system without getting distracted by implementation details.

DFDs are drawn in levels, starting from the most abstract (Level 0 or Context Diagram) and progressively decomposing processes into more detailed sub-diagrams (Level 1, Level 2, etc.). This hierarchical decomposition is one of the most powerful aspects of DFDs because it allows the analyst to present the system at any desired level of detail without overwhelming the reader.

Level 0 / Context Diagram: This is the highest-level DFD, showing the entire system as a single process (usually numbered 0) with all external entities and the data flows between them and the system. The context diagram establishes the boundary of the system—it shows what is inside the system (the single process) and what is outside (the external entities). No data stores are shown in the context diagram because the system is treated as a black box at this level. The purpose of the context diagram is to provide a quick overview of the system's interactions with its environment, and it is often the first diagram drawn during structured analysis.

Level 1 DFD: This diagram decomposes the single process from the context diagram into its major sub-processes (typically 3 to 7 processes), showing the data stores that connect them and the data flows between processes, data stores, and external entities. Each sub-process is numbered (1.0, 2.0, 3.0, etc.) to indicate its position in the hierarchy. The Level 1 DFD must be balanced with the context diagram—every data flow that enters or leaves the system in the context diagram must also appear in the Level 1 DFD, entering or leaving the same external entity.

Level 2 DFD: Each process in the Level 1 DFD can be further decomposed into a Level 2 DFD if it is complex enough to warrant additional detail. The numbering continues hierarchically: process 2.0 in Level 1 decomposes into processes 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, etc. in Level 2. Again, balance must be maintained: every data flow entering or leaving process 2.0 in Level 1 must also appear entering or leaving the corresponding process in Level 2.

Common Student Confusion: DFD Balancing

Students often forget to maintain balance between levels. The rule is simple: if a data flow enters process 2.0 from an external entity at Level 1, then at Level 2, that same data flow must enter one of the sub-processes (2.1, 2.2, etc.) from the same external entity. No data flow can appear or disappear between levels. If you find yourself adding or removing data flows between levels, you have made an error. This is the single most important quality check for DFDs.

Data Dictionary

A Data Dictionary (also called a data repository or data definition) is a centralized collection of descriptions of all data elements, data flows, data stores, and processes in a system. While DFDs show the flow and transformation of data graphically, the data dictionary provides the detailed textual descriptions that the DFD cannot convey: the exact composition of each data flow, the format of each data element, the valid ranges and default values, and the relationships between data elements. Together, the DFD and the data dictionary form a complete specification—neither alone is sufficient.

The data dictionary defines data elements using a standard notation. The most commonly used notations in Indian university exams are:

Data Dictionary Notation
  • = — "is composed of" (defines the composition of a data element)
  • + — "and" (sequential composition, e.g., Name = First_Name + Last_Name)
  • [ ] — "either/or" (choice between alternatives, e.g., Gender = [M | F])
  • { } — "repetition" (one or more occurrences, e.g., Order = {Item})
  • ( ) — "optional" (may or may not be present, e.g., Phone = Area_Code + Number + (Extension))
  • ** — "comment" (annotation, e.g., *valid range 1-999*)
  • @ — "key field" (unique identifier, e.g., @Employee_ID)

For example, a data dictionary entry for the Employee Management System might look like:

Employee_Record = @Employee_ID + First_Name + Last_Name + Date_of_Birth + Address + Phone + Department + Designation + Date_of_Joining + Salary
Employee_ID = *unique 6-digit identifier*
First_Name = *alphabetic string, 2-30 characters*
Salary = *numeric, range 8000-999999*
Department = [IT | HR | Finance | Marketing | Operations]
Leave_Request = @Request_ID + @Employee_ID + Leave_Type + Start_Date + End_Date + Reason + Status
Leave_Type = [Casual | Sick | Earned | Maternity]
Status = [Pending | Approved | Rejected]

Exam Tip: Drawing DFDs in Exams

When drawing DFDs in university exams, keep these points in mind: (1) Always start with a context diagram before Level 1. (2) Use the standard symbols—do not invent your own. (3) Label every data flow with the specific data being transferred, not just "data" or "info." (4) Number processes hierarchically (0, 1.0, 2.0, etc.). (5) Maintain balance between levels—every data flow in the context diagram must appear in Level 1. (6) Data stores do NOT appear in the context diagram. (7) All processes must have at least one input and one output data flow. (8) External entities should not directly connect to each other—they communicate only through the system.

VIII Part Eight

Non-Traditional Analysis Tools: FSM, Statecharts, Petri Nets

The tools discussed so far—DFDs, decision tables, decision trees—are excellent for representing data flow and discrete logic, but they fall short when the system's behavior is fundamentally governed by states and transitions—situations where the system's response to an input depends not just on the input itself, but on what the system was doing when the input arrived. A traffic light controller, a network protocol, a vending machine, a cardiac monitor: these are systems where the concept of "state" is primary, and where the flow of data is secondary to the rules that govern how the system moves from one state to another. For such systems, we need modeling tools that make states and transitions explicit, and the three most important such tools are Finite State Machines (FSMs), Statecharts, and Petri Nets.

Finite State Machine (FSM)

A Finite State Machine (also called a finite state automaton) is a mathematical model of computation consisting of a finite number of states, transitions between those states, and actions associated with the transitions. The core idea is beautifully simple: at any given moment, the system is in exactly one of its defined states; when an event occurs (an input is received), the system evaluates the current state and the input, transitions to a new state (which may be the same state), and optionally produces an output. This transition is typically described by a transition function that maps (current state, input) pairs to (next state, output) pairs.

The formal definition of an FSM is a quintuple M = (Q, Σ, δ, q₀, F), where Q is the finite set of states, Σ is the finite set of input symbols (the alphabet), δ is the transition function (δ: Q × Σ → Q), q₀ is the initial state (the state the system enters when it starts), and F is the set of final or accepting states. In the context of requirements modeling, the "accepting states" often correspond to completed operations or safe shutdown states, and the transition function encodes the system's behavioral specification.

Figure 6 — Finite State Machine: Simple Door Controller

The primary limitation of classical FSMs is a phenomenon known as state explosion. If a system has multiple independent aspects (say, a door that can be open/closed and locked/unlocked), the number of states in the FSM multiplies: we need states for Closed-Locked, Closed-Unlocked, Open-Locked (possibly invalid), and Open-Unlocked. For systems with many independent dimensions, the total number of states grows exponentially, making the FSM diagram incomprehensible. This limitation motivated the development of Statecharts.

Statecharts

Statecharts, invented by David Harel in 1987, extend FSMs with three crucial mechanisms that address the state explosion problem: hierarchy (superstates and substates), concurrency (orthogonal regions), and broadcast communication (events that cause transitions across multiple regions simultaneously). Statecharts are the behavioral foundation of the Unified Modeling Language (UML) state machine diagrams, and they are the standard notation for modeling reactive and real-time systems in modern software engineering.

Hierarchy (Superstates): A superstate is a state that contains other states (substates). If several states share the same outgoing transitions, they can be grouped into a superstate, and the transitions can be attached to the superstate instead, significantly reducing clutter. For example, if a system has states "Playing," "Paused," and "Stopped," and all three need a transition to "Error" on the event "hardware_failure," we can group "Playing," "Paused," and "Stopped" into a superstate "Active" and attach the "hardware_failure" transition to "Active."

Concurrency (Orthogonal Regions): When a system has independent parallel aspects, Statecharts represent them as orthogonal regions within a state, separated by dashed lines. Each region has its own set of states and transitions, and the system's overall state is the Cartesian product of the states of each region—but crucially, the diagram does not need to enumerate every combination. This is the primary mechanism for combating state explosion: instead of drawing N × M states for two independent components with N and M states respectively, we draw N + M states in two parallel regions.

Figure 7 — Statechart with Orthogonal Regions: CD Player (Disc Mechanism || Display)

Petri Nets

While FSMs and Statecharts model sequential behavior, Petri Nets are specifically designed to model concurrent, asynchronous, distributed, and parallel systems. Invented by Carl Adam Petri in 1962, Petri Nets provide a graphical and mathematical framework for representing systems where multiple activities can occur simultaneously, where synchronization between parallel processes is required, and where resource sharing and contention must be modeled. These characteristics make Petri Nets particularly valuable for modeling workflow systems, communication protocols, manufacturing processes, and distributed algorithms.

A Petri Net consists of four types of elements: Places (drawn as circles) represent conditions, states, or resources; Transitions (drawn as rectangles or bars) represent events or actions that change the state; Arcs (directed edges) connect places to transitions and transitions to places, specifying the flow; and Tokens (drawn as dots inside places) represent the current state of the system. The distribution of tokens across places at any given moment is called the marking of the net. A transition is enabled (ready to fire) when every input place of that transition contains at least the number of tokens specified by the arc weight (typically one). When an enabled transition fires, it consumes tokens from its input places and produces tokens in its output places, changing the marking and thus the state of the system.

Figure 8 — Petri Net: Order Processing with Synchronization

The critical power of Petri Nets is their ability to model synchronization and concurrency naturally. In the diagram above, transition T3 ("Ship Order") has two input places: P2 ("Payment Verified") and P3 ("Stock Available"). T3 can only fire when both places contain tokens—meaning the order can only be shipped when payment has been verified AND the item is in stock. This AND-synchronization is cumbersome to express in classical FSMs but is native to Petri Nets. Similarly, Petri Nets naturally model conflict (when two transitions are enabled but firing one disables the other) and concurrency (when two transitions can fire independently in any order), making them the preferred tool for specifying the behavior of systems where parallelism and resource contention are central concerns.

System Modeling: DFD Problems and Solutions

Examinations in Software Engineering frequently test a student's ability to translate a natural-language problem description into structured graphical models. The key to solving these problems is not artistic skill but systematic decomposition: identify external entities, identify major processes, identify data stores, and trace the data flows between them. In this section, we work through two of the most commonly asked DFD problems from the PYQ bank, demonstrating the step-by-step reasoning that leads to a correct diagram.

Problem 1: Coffee Vending Machine

"A coffee vending machine serves coffee to customers. A customer can choose a type of coffee, supply the amount, and get served. Each coffee is prepared by adding units of hot water, coffee powder, milk, and sugar. The recipe for each coffee is stored."

Before drawing the DFD, let us identify the elements systematically. The external entity is the Customer. The major processes are: (1) Accept Selection and Payment, (2) Prepare Coffee, (3) Dispense Coffee and Change. The data stores are: (1) Recipe Database (stores recipes for each coffee type), (2) Inventory Database (tracks quantities of ingredients). The data flows are: Coffee Selection, Amount Paid, Recipe, Ingredient Quantities, Coffee, Change.

How to Structure a 10-Mark Descriptive Answer

University examiners award marks for structure, coverage, and precision. A 10-mark answer typically requires 2-3 pages of handwritten content. Use this template:

  1. Introduction / Definition (2 marks): Define the core concept precisely. Use the formal, textbook-style definition first, then provide a one-sentence intuitive explanation. This demonstrates that you know the academic terminology.
  2. Detailed Explanation (4 marks): Break the concept into its components. Use sub-headings or numbered points. Explain each component with a concrete example. If the concept has a process, list the steps sequentially. If it has types, classify them explicitly.
  3. Diagram or Table (2 marks): Draw a labeled diagram (DFD, FSM, classification chart) or present a comparison table. Diagrams must have a title, labels on all elements, and a brief written explanation of what the diagram shows. Never draw a diagram without accompanying text.
  4. Real-World Example or Application (1 mark): Connect the concept to a specific, recognizable system. Avoid vague examples—"a banking system" is weak; "the NEFT transaction processing system at SBI" is strong.
  5. Conclusion / Significance (1 mark): Summarize why this concept matters in the broader context of software engineering. Avoid generic filler; instead, connect back to the cost of errors, the quality of the final system, or the relationship to other phases of development.

Viva Voce: Rapid-Fire Questions

Viva
What is the single most important property of a good SRS and why?
Verifiability. If a requirement cannot be tested, you cannot determine whether the implemented system meets it. All other qualities (completeness, consistency) are necessary but insufficient if the requirement is fundamentally untestable. "The system shall be user-friendly" fails verifiability; "A new user shall complete registration in under 3 minutes without help" passes it.
Viva
Why are data stores not shown in a Level 0 (context) DFD?
Because the Level 0 DFD treats the entire system as a single black-box process. Data stores are internal to the system; they are revealed only when the process is decomposed at Level 1. The context diagram's purpose is to show the system's boundary and its interactions with external entities, not its internal structure.
Viva
Give one example of a volatile requirement and explain why it is volatile.
"The GST rate for restaurant billing shall be 5%." This is volatile because it is a consequential/compatibility requirement dependent on government tax policy, which can change with new legislation. Enduring requirements, by contrast, derive from the fundamental nature of the domain (e.g., "every bill shall have a unique invoice number").
Viva
What problem do Statecharts solve that ordinary FSMs cannot?
State explosion. In a flat FSM, independent aspects of the system multiply the number of states (N × M for two aspects with N and M states). Statecharts solve this through hierarchy (grouping states into superstates to share transitions) and orthogonality (representing independent aspects as parallel regions, reducing the drawn states to N + M).
Viva
What does "DFD balancing" mean?
Balancing means that every data flow at the boundary of a process at one level must appear at the boundary of its decomposition at the next level. If a context diagram shows a data flow "Order Details" from Customer to the system, then the Level 1 DFD must also show "Order Details" entering from Customer to one of the sub-processes. No data can appear or disappear between levels.
